# Six enticing versions — and the cost slot, developed

*Sep 7, night. Built against Joan's test: not "matters of fact, this is what happens," but **"what are the things that are going to make me go, wow, now that's a film."***

---

## The thing no version has said yet

**He stumbles on the funeral of the man he killed, and the mourners are his own people, practising the faith he shares.**

He is the murderer at the funeral, and the funeral is his own church. Every version so far has said he "reckons" or "confronts" — abstractions. The enticing fact is his *position*: he cannot grieve, cannot join, cannot claim the faith being practised six feet from him, because he made the body.

That is the collision. Everything below is built on it.

---

## The cost slot, developed

The weakest slot, because every entry in it was an abstraction. Ranked by how much a programmer would want to see the scene it implies.

### Tier 1 — the cost is a right he has lost

- **the right to mourn** · he is the only man there with no right to mourn
- **the right to grieve** · is asked to grieve a man he killed
- **the right to claim the faith** · finds it is a faith he no longer has the right to claim
- **a place in the ground beside them** · costs him the right to be buried among his own
- **the right to pray with them** · he can stand at the grave but not at the prayer

*Why these work: each one names a specific action he is barred from, in a scene the reader can already see. "Humanity" is a noun; "the right to mourn" is a closed door.*

### Tier 2 — the cost is what he has become

- has hardened him past recognition
- consumes another piece of his humanity *(the crux line — his)*
- has made him unrecognisable to his own dead
- has made him the thing his people fear
- costs him the man he was meant to become *(his)*
- drags him further from the ancestors who would know him

### Tier 3 — the cost is his standing (weaker: abstract, and "estranged" is flat)

- leaves him estranged from his own people *(his — flagged by him as not enticing)*
- costs him his place among his own
- belongs a little more to the men who own him *(his — flagged: "not that enticing")*

### Trait words he picked out

**his will to live** · **what he is willing to do to stay alive** · his physical dominance · the violence that keeps him alive

**Verbs he picked out:** hardens · consumes · drags him further

## The six

### 1. The collision, said plainly — the murderer at the funeral · 42 words

> In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter survives by killing his own. When he stumbles upon his fallen opponent's burial ceremony, held in the faith of their shared ancestors, he finds he is **the only man there with no right to mourn.**

*"Survives by killing his own" — the phrase he called enticing — plus the position no version has named. The last eight words are the film.*

### 2. His C opening, with the cost made concrete · 52 words

> In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter's physical dominance keeps him alive in the fighting pits and **costs him the right to be buried among his own.** At his fallen opponent's burial ceremony, held in the faith of their shared ancestors, he must reckon with the man he was meant to become.

*His C opening, which he liked, with "estranged from his own people" replaced by the thing that scene is actually about: he is watching a burial he will never be given.*

### 3. What he is willing to do · 53 words

> In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter survives by killing his own. **What he is willing to do to stay alive has hardened him past recognition** — until he stumbles upon his fallen opponent's burial ceremony, held in the faith of their shared ancestors, and **is asked to grieve a man he killed.**

*His two favourite phrases from the bank, in one sentence. "Is asked to grieve a man he killed" is the most uncomfortable ending on the page — which is the point.*

### 4. The crux line, with the cost landing on the rite · 65 words

> In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter's **will to live** has made him the deadliest man on the plantation. With every victory **the violence that keeps him alive consumes another piece of his humanity**, until he stumbles upon his fallen opponent's burial ceremony, held in the faith of their shared ancestors, and **finds it is a faith he no longer has the right to claim.**

*His crux line intact — "we're right there" — with "costs" swapped for "consumes" (his word) and an ending that makes the abstraction land on the specific rite in front of him.*

### 5. The owners' sport — the horror stated flat · 52 words

> In 1834 South Carolina, **enslaved men are made to fight to the death for their owners' sport, and one of them has never lost.** When he stumbles upon his fallen opponent's burial ceremony, held in the faith of their shared ancestors, he must reckon with the man he was meant to become.

*Em & Selma's move: state the world's rule, let the reader infer the story. "And one of them has never lost" is the hook — nine words that make him a monster and a victim at once.*

### 6. Shortest — the collision and nothing else · 40 words

> In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter is **made to kill his own for his owner's sport.** He stumbles upon his fallen opponent's burial ceremony, held in the faith of their shared ancestors, and **must mourn a man he killed.**

*No cost clause at all. The collision does the work: the whole moral disaster is in "must mourn a man he killed."*

---

## Which is most enticing

**1** and **6** are the two that would stop a programmer, because both end on the impossible position rather than on a reckoning. **3** is the most uncomfortable and the most memorable. **4** keeps his crux line, which he says is nearly there — it is the safest choice and the longest.

The line to steal if only one thing survives: **"the only man there with no right to mourn."**
