## Director statement — Luke's edits, Sep 7

Paragraph 1 comes in two versions: **A** is exactly the edits Luke dictated; **B** is the better-flowing draft he asked for, which fixes the dangling "it" (three sentences after "persecution") by naming what the microaggressions are. B adds no facts; the one optional image is bracketed for Jordan.

**P1 — version A (Luke's edits only)**

> Growing up, I was always one of the youngest in my year, but my height made me one of the tallest in the class and often the biggest in the room. As I got older, I started noticing how my mere presence was seen as either a point of praise or persecution. My physicality, tied so closely to my skin, made me seem more menacing, more dangerous than the other boys—even those bigger and stronger than I was. It's never anything overt, but small, incessant microaggressions. Death by a million needles. These interactions have shaped me throughout my life, leaving me hyper-aware of myself in spaces—even in this country I call home. This experience, this reality, is the plight of all Black men in America.

**P1 — version B (flow draft)**

> Growing up, I was always one of the youngest in my year, but my height made me one of the tallest in the class and often the biggest in the room. As I got older, I started noticing how my mere presence was read as either a point of praise or persecution. My physicality, tied so closely to my skin, made me seem more menacing, more dangerous than the other boys—even those bigger and stronger than I was. The persecution was never anything overt. It came as small, incessant microaggressions **[— a glance, a step back, a hand that moved toward a bag —]** death by a million needles. Those interactions have shaped me throughout my life, leaving me hyper-aware of myself in every room I enter—even in the country I call home. This experience, this reality, is the plight of Black men in America.

What B changes and why: "seen as" → "read as" (presence is read, not seen); "It's never anything overt" → "The persecution was never anything overt" (the referent is now in the sentence); "small, incessant microaggressions" and "death by a million needles" joined into one movement; "in spaces" → "in every room I enter" (the room was set up in the first sentence, so the paragraph closes where it opened); "all Black men" → "Black men" (the claim lands harder without the absolute — Jordan's call).

**P2 — unchanged, Luke: "this paragraph is good"**

> Killer of Men is an expression of pain—absolute brutality and suffering. But it's also a story of resilience, culture, and spirituality. This story has kept me awake at night, terrified of what I'm putting on the screen.

**P3 — Mandingo, with Luke's edits**

> The concept of Mandingo fighting has always fascinated me. It's a strange, bastardized idea in media and literature. To me, it represents many things: the commercialization of sports and entertainment, the myth of Black-on-Black crime, the portrayal of savagery in history and in the media. It has always been used to showcase the animalistic "Black brute"; rarely, if ever, has an audience been invited into the subjective perspective of the fighters themselves. What are their lives like? What do they hold dear? What keeps a man going when he's put in a situation like that?

**P4 — the setting, with Luke's edits**

> Setting this film during the period of American slavery, rather than in the present day or during the 1960s, allows me to examine the deep-rooted history of this aggression. Visually, I want the fight scenes to reflect the physical sports Black men often participate in—body slams, boxing, football tackles—to show history repeating itself through various forms of expression. The period also gave me the chance to explore my heritage. With American chattel slavery at its peak and the transatlantic slave trade bringing thousands of enslaved people ashore every year, we can examine the themes we still see today as they were in the darkest hour of African Americans.

**⚠ A history check before this goes to Jordan.** The United States outlawed the import of enslaved people from Africa in 1808. By the 1830s the *domestic* trade was at its peak (the Upper South selling south and west), while the transatlantic trade into the US continued only as illegal smuggling — real, but not "thousands every year." A programmer or juror who knows the period will catch "the transatlantic slave trade bringing thousands ashore every year" in 1830s South Carolina. Two honest ways to keep the idea:

> …With American chattel slavery at its peak, a generation after the Atlantic trade was outlawed but with Africa still living in the memory and rites of the enslaved, we can examine the themes we still see today as they were in the darkest hour of African Americans.

or, if the film's premise depends on new arrivals (the Lowcountry did see illegal landings, and Gullah communities kept the strongest African retention in the country):

> …With American chattel slavery at its peak and enslaved Africans still being smuggled ashore in defiance of the ban, we can examine the themes we still see today as they were in the darkest hour of African Americans.

The name Luke was reaching for: **the transatlantic slave trade**, also called **the triangular trade** (Europe → West Africa → the Americas → Europe). Either is correct; "transatlantic" is the one historians use.

**P5 — spirituality, with Luke's edit, then P7 unchanged**

> A critical piece of this film is the spirituality of the enslaved at the time—their mix of cultures, beliefs and religions that slowly formed what we now recognise as Black culture. Killer of Men has been a grueling process—one that's forced me to question a lot of myself as a man and as a storyteller. This is my Oriki, my folktale, my poem. I won't shy away from telling difficult stories or giving a voice to the voiceless. I will honor my ancestors and my culture in everything I do.
