## Branch A — the event, then the reckoning

*The original shape: he stumbles on the ceremony; it ignites a reckoning. Verdicts so far: the original is "way too wordy"; "roots, guilt, faith and violence" is "a big list… just buzzwords."*

Luke's versions, in order:

- "Killer of Men follows Mace, an enslaved man forced into Mandingo fights. After killing an opponent, he stumbles upon the burial of the man he killed, igniting a reckoning with guilt, faith, and the violence shaping his life and identity." *(the page — too wordy)*
- "Killer of Men, 1830 South Carolina. An enslaved man, forced into a world of underground prizefighting, stumbles upon the burial ceremony of the opponent he has just killed, igniting a reckoning with guilt, faith and the violence shaping his life and identity." *(v3)*
- "A South Carolina plantation, 1830. An enslaved man forced into a world of underground prizefighting stumbles upon his fallen opponent's burial ceremony after a brutal fight to the death. The encounter ignites a reckoning with his ancestral roots, guilt, faith and the violence that has shaped his life and identity." *(v4, current page draft)*
- "South Carolina plantation. An enslaved man is forced into the deadly world of Mandingo prizefighting. After killing his opponent, he finds himself confronted by the victim's burial ceremony."
- **"Deep in the heat of a Carolina plantation, an enslaved prizefighter stumbles upon the burial ceremony of an opponent he has just killed. It ignites a reckoning between his life of violence and the spirituality of his ancestral past."** — *"I really like that so far."*
- "Killer of Men is set in the heat of a South Carolina plantation. An enslaved man forced to fight to survive, fight or die. He encounters the burial ceremony of his fallen opponent. This confrontation with his own people ignites a reckoning, beginning with his spiritual ancestry and the violent brutality that has shaped him."
- "An enslaved man in 1830 South Carolina. His natural athleticism forces him into a life where he must fight or die for the entertainment of others. After defeating the opponent in a deadly prizefight, he encounters the burial ceremony of his fallen opponent."
- "After claiming victory in a deadly prizefight, he encounters the burial ceremony of his fallen opponent. There he is given a glimpse… It ignites a reckoning."

**Branch A, best current line — his, with 1834:**

> Deep in the heat of a Carolina plantation, an enslaved prizefighter stumbles upon the burial ceremony of an opponent he has just killed. It ignites a reckoning between his life of violence and the spirituality of his ancestral past. *(37 words)*

## Branch B — the same brutality that keeps him alive is what it costs him

*The Raging Bull rebuild. Luke: "a much better hook for our story… I love that kind of dichotomy. Keeps it both active and character-driven."*

Luke's versions, in order:

- "In 1830 South Carolina. An enslaved man is forced to fight to survive. But the brutality that has kept him alive has cost him… has caused him to forget, to forsake, his ancestral past."
- "1830 South Carolina. An enslaved prizefighter, forced to survive a brutal reality where he must kill or be killed for others' entertainment." — *"That's pretty good."*
- **"In 1830 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter is forced to survive in a world where he must win or die for the entertainment of others. But the rage and violence that have kept him alive may also prove to strip him of his humanity. When he encounters the burial ceremony of his fallen opponent, he is forced to reckon with the man he has become, and [something] with his ancestral past."** — *"I really like where that's at."*
- "Killer of Men is set in the heat of a South Carolina plantation where an enslaved prizefighter is doomed to fight to survive, to win or die. But the same brutality that has kept him alive costs him something greater than death." — *"kind of cheesy but I still like it"*
- "But the same brutality that has kept him alive threatens to strip him of his humanity." — *"sounds cheesy when I say it… there's probably a better version"*
- "He encounters the burial ceremony of his fallen opponent, held in the faith of their shared ancestors, and is reconnected with his spiritual heritage. He is confronted with the cost of his brutal way of life."
- "He stumbles upon this fallen opponent's burial ceremony, held in the faith of his spiritual home, or ancestral home."
- "In 1830 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter is forced to survive in a world where he must win or die."
- On the ending "forced to reckon with the man he has become": *"okay. It's an okay ending."*
- On "costs him a little more of himself each time he wins": *"an interesting concept. Let's expand on that."*

**Branch B, assembled from Luke's words:**

> In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter must win or die for the entertainment of others. But the same brutality that has kept him alive is taking the rest of him. When he encounters his fallen opponent's burial, held in the faith of their shared ancestors, he is forced to reckon with the man he has become. *(52 — over target; the cost clause and the ending are the two places to cut and the two places still to decide)*

**Branch B with the "each time he wins" engine** (assembled, Luke's phrases + the film's line):

> In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter must win or die for sport, and every win costs him a little more of the man he might have been. Then he stumbles upon the dead man's burial and is shown the god he would have been. *(44)*

## Branch C — the spirits confront him, and he must decide

*Opened this turn. The ancestors become agents; the ending is a choice.*

Luke's versions, in order:

- **"In 1830 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter is thrust into a world where he must kill to survive. But when he stumbles upon the dead man's burial ceremony, held in their shared faith, he is confronted by the spirits of his ancestors — boom — and must decide whether he will continue to participate in the violence that has kept him alive."**
- "An enslaved prizefighter is forced into a world where he must kill for sport."
- On "which of the two men will walk out of the barn": *"interesting, but it would need to be specific that it's not which of the two fighters"* — the two men are both him.

**Branch C, assembled from Luke's words:**

> In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter must kill to survive. But when he stumbles upon the dead man's burial, held in their shared faith, he is confronted by the spirits of his ancestors and must decide whether the fighter or the man walks out of the barn. *(46)*

**Branch C with the film's own line** (assembled; the strongest candidate on the table):

> In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter must kill to survive. But when he stumbles upon the dead man's burial, held in their shared faith, the spirits of his ancestors show him the man he would have been, had the ships never come. *(42)*

### The cost clause — "the same brutality that has kept him alive…"

Better than "strip him of his humanity":

- …is taking the rest of him
- …is burying the man he might have been *(the burial is already in the sentence)*
- …is killing the man inside the fighter
- …is turning him into the thing they paid to see *(the myth, made personal)*
- …is hollowing him out
- …has been eating him alive
- …has cost him the part of himself that could still mourn
- …at a price greater than death *(his; cheesy, liked)*

"Costs him a little more of himself each time he wins," expanded:

- Every fight he wins costs him a little more of the man he might have been.
- He has never lost a fight, and every win has cost him something the barn cannot see.
- The more he wins, the less of him there is.
- Each win keeps him alive and takes a piece of him with it.
- Winning is the only way to stay alive, and every win buries a little more of him.
- He wins every fight. It is the winning that is killing him.
