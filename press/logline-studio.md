## 1. Raging Bull, taken apart

> In the 1940s Bronx, middleweight boxer Jake LaMotta fights his way to the championship. But the rage and violence that propel him to the top threaten to destroy everything outside the ring—including his marriage, and his relationship with his brother and manager, Joey.

*(MUBI, distributor-supplied. 43 words.)*

| Beat | Words | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | *In the 1940s Bronx* | Decade and place in four words, prepositional. No sentence spent on it |
| Protagonist as role, then name | *middleweight boxer Jake LaMotta* | The job comes before the name, so the name arrives already meaning something |
| The win, as a finished fact | *fights his way to the championship* | The entire plot of winning is one verb phrase. It gives the win away. The win is not the story |
| The pivot | *But* | Sentence two opens on the reversal |
| The trait, credited first | *the rage and violence that propel him to the top* | Names the exact quality and gives it the win before charging it with the loss |
| The arena of loss | *threaten to destroy everything outside the ring* | Inside the ring the trait is legal; outside it, it is ruin. One spatial line divides the two lives |
| The casualties | *including his marriage, and his relationship with his brother and manager, Joey* | Abstraction (everything), then two concrete instances, ending on a person's name |

**The mechanism.** A *trait* with two opposite consequences, not an *event*. The reader can run it in their head: whatever makes him win will make him lose. A machine is more interesting than a feeling.

**Mace's version.** The trait: the brutality that keeps him alive in the barn. "Outside the ring": his people, and the faith of their ancestors. The burial is where the two lives meet — the moment the trait becomes unbearable.

**What not to take.** The biography and the given-away ending. Killer of Men is thirteen minutes and one night. Take the *But* and the named cost.

### Branch B, assembled from Luke's own words

Nothing added except connective tissue; every phrase is his from the idea box.

**B-full** — his three beats intact (56 words, three sentences; Sundance allows 75):

> In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter must win or die for the entertainment of others. But the same brutality that has kept him alive is stripping him of his humanity. When he encounters the burial ceremony of his fallen opponent, held in the faith of their shared ancestors, he is forced to reckon with the man he has become.

**B-two** — the *But* and the ceremony in one sentence (44 words):

> In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter must win or die for the entertainment of others. But when he encounters his fallen opponent's burial ceremony, held in the faith of their shared ancestors, he must reckon with the man the fighting has made of him.

**B-tight** — the cost clause carried by "and", the ceremony as the last image (42 words):

> 1834, South Carolina. An enslaved prizefighter wins or dies for the entertainment of others, and the brutality that keeps him alive is taking the rest of him. Then he walks into his fallen opponent's burial, held in the faith of their shared ancestors.

**Where each one still needs a decision:** the cost clause (§4) and the ending (§5). Those are the two places the words go worn.

## 4. The cost clause — ways to say "it cost him his soul" without saying soul

Luke's question: *"The same brutality that has kept him alive… what's the best way to say cost him his soul, forgot how to be human?"* The bank, each marked for wear.

| Clause | Wear | Note |
|---|---|---|
| …is stripping him of his humanity | worn | His own; "humanity" is the abstraction programmers have read a thousand times |
| …is taking the rest of him | clean | Concrete; implies the owner already has the body |
| …costs him a little more of himself each time he wins | clean | The cost is per win — the win *is* the loss, which is the Raging Bull machine exactly |
| …is hollowing him out | clean | Physical; short |
| …has made him a stranger to himself | half-worn | Familiar phrase, but earned here — he no longer knows the man in the barn |
| …has made a killer of him, and the killer is all that is left | clean | Says the title without saying it |
| …has taken everything the barn cannot see | clean | The barn sees the body; what it takes is unseen |
| …is turning him into the thing they paid to see | clean | The Mandingo myth, made personal: he is becoming the spectacle |
| …has cost him the part of himself that could still mourn | clean | Points straight at the burial — the cost is the capacity to grieve, and the ceremony is grief |
| …at a price greater than death | cheesy, liked | His; keep as a fallback if the sentence needs a landing |
| …at a price the barn never names | clean | The cousin of the one above without the cheese |
| …is unmaking him | clean | Two words; almost too fast |

The two I would actually put in a sentence: **"costs him a little more of himself each time he wins"** (Branch B's engine in nine words) and **"has cost him the part of himself that could still mourn"** (it makes the burial the inevitable next line).

## 5. Endings that are not worn and still lift

The rule from the corpus: do not end on *freedom*, *roots*, *past*, *soul*, *faith* or *humanity* standing alone. The ending can still rise — it just has to land on something a reader can see, or on the ancestors as *agents* rather than as a noun.

| Ending | What it lands on | Note |
|---|---|---|
| …he is forced to reckon with the man he has become. | a stock phrase | His; serviceable; "the man he has become" is semi-worn |
| …and must reckon with what the fighting has made of him. | the fighting | Liked; cleaner than the one above because "the fighting" is a thing |
| …and the rites are for him too. | the rite reaching him | Five words; the ancestors claim the living man as well as the dead one |
| …and hears his own name among the mourned. | a sound | The most concrete; implies he is already one of the dead unless he changes |
| …and the ancestors he has never met come looking for him. | the ancestors as agents | Ancestry without the word "ancestral"; active |
| …where his ancestors have kept a place for him. | a place | Inspiring in the plain sense — there is somewhere for him |
| …and begins to remember who he was before the first fight. | a memory | Ends on "fight", the film's own word; the lift is the remembering |
| …and must decide which of the two men will walk out of the barn. | a choice | Branch B's machine resolved as a decision; ends on the barn |
| …and learns the dead man's gods are his own. | the gods | "His own" does the "his own people" work |
| …and finds a fight the barn never taught him. | a different fight | Turns the title's violence toward the spiritual struggle |
| …and is offered a way back that will cost him the only thing that has kept him alive. | the cost, again | The full Raging Bull dilemma in one clause; long |

## 6. The body — his physicality, from the director's statement

Jordan's statement opens on his own size: *"my physicality, tied so closely to my skin, made me seem more menacing, more dangerous."* Luke wants that in the logline. Lines that carry it without an adjective:

- an enslaved man whose body is worth more in the barn than in the field
- a man built for the fields and sold to the ring
- an enslaved man whose size makes him a prize before he has thrown a punch
- a man whose body has always been read as a threat, and is now priced as one
- an enslaved man whose strength is the only thing his owner values, and the only thing the barn will take from him

The first and fourth are the strongest: each states the economics of his body in one clause, and each is the director's own theme.

## Appendix A — the six originals (Sep 7, earlier). Luke: "none of these six are very good."

Kept for the record. Their review passes are in the git history.

1. On a South Carolina plantation in 1830, an enslaved man's body belongs to the owner who bets on him in the pit. After he kills his opponent, the dead man's burial rites come for the rest of him.
2. In the heat of a Carolina plantation, an enslaved man is sent into the pit to fight one of his own, and wins. At the burial, held in the faith of their ancestors, the winner is one of them.
3. Mace is the best fighter on a South Carolina plantation in 1830, which means he kills for the men who own him. Then he stumbles upon the Yoruba rites being held for his last opponent.
4. On the plantations of 1830s South Carolina, enslaved men are made to fight each other to the death for their owners' wagers. One of them wins, and stumbles into the loser's burial, held in the faith they share.
5. 1830, South Carolina. An enslaved man is made to fight to the death for the men who own him, and he is good at it. Then he stumbles upon the burial rites of the man he has just killed.
6. In the fighting pits of a Carolina plantation, an enslaved man stays alive by killing. But when he stumbles upon the dead man's burial rites, the violence that keeps him alive becomes the thing he has to answer for.
