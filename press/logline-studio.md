## 1. Raging Bull, taken apart

> In the 1940s Bronx, middleweight boxer Jake LaMotta fights his way to the championship. But the rage and violence that propel him to the top threaten to destroy everything outside the ring—including his marriage, and his relationship with his brother and manager, Joey.

*(MUBI, distributor-supplied. 43 words.)*

| Beat | Words | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | *In the 1940s Bronx* | Decade and place in four words, prepositional. No sentence spent on it |
| Protagonist as role, then name | *middleweight boxer Jake LaMotta* | The job comes before the name, so the name arrives already meaning something |
| The win, as a finished fact | *fights his way to the championship* | The entire plot of winning is one verb phrase. It gives the win away. The win is not the story |
| The pivot | *But* | Sentence two opens on the reversal — the same "But" that 14 of the 30 KOM-adjacent loglines use |
| The trait, credited first | *the rage and violence that propel him to the top* | Names the exact quality and gives it the win before charging it with the loss |
| The arena of loss | *threaten to destroy everything outside the ring* | Inside the ring the trait is legal; outside it, it is ruin. One spatial line divides the two lives |
| The casualties | *including his marriage, and his relationship with his brother and manager, Joey* | Abstraction (everything), then two concrete instances, ending on a person's name |

**The mechanism.** It is built on a *trait* with two opposite consequences, not on an *event*. The reader can run it in their head: whatever makes him win will make him lose. That is a machine, and a machine is more interesting than a feeling.

**How it differs from the shape Killer of Men has been using.** Yours is built on an event (he stumbles on the ceremony) that triggers an internal state (a reckoning). Raging Bull promises a mechanism; yours promises a feeling. Both are legitimate. The corpus says 85% of shorts state the cost — Raging Bull's second sentence *is* a cost statement.

**What Mace's version of the trait would be.** The ferocity that keeps him alive in the fights. What is "outside the ring" for him: his people, and the faith of his ancestors. The burial ceremony is the moment the trait becomes unbearable — it is where the two lives meet.

**Why not simply adopt it.** Raging Bull spans years and gives away the ending; Killer of Men is thirteen minutes and one night, and its shape is an encounter. Take the *But* and the named cost. Leave the biography.

## 3. Six originals — written blind to the existing version, reviewed twice

**Brief I set myself:** the facts of the film only (an enslaved man, a South Carolina plantation, 1830, fights to the death for his owner, kills, stumbles on the dead man's burial rites, the faith of his ancestors, a reckoning). Every line must state the cost or the irony. Under forty words. Present tense. One or two sentences. No dash, no colon. End on something you can see, not on an abstraction.

### First drafts

1. On a South Carolina plantation in 1830, an enslaved man's body belongs to the owner who bets on him in the pit. After he kills his opponent, the dead man's burial rites lay claim to his soul.
2. In the fighting pits of 1830 South Carolina, an enslaved man survives by killing his own. The night he stumbles upon his victim's burial rites, the faith of his ancestors asks him to account for it.
3. Mace is the best fighter on a South Carolina plantation in 1830, which means he kills for the men who own him. When he stumbles upon the Yoruba rites being held for his last opponent, the ancestors he has never met come looking for him.
4. On the plantations of 1830s South Carolina, enslaved men are made to fight each other to the death for their owners' wagers. One of them wins, and follows the body to a burial held in the faith of their ancestors.
5. 1830, South Carolina. An enslaved man is made to fight to the death for the entertainment of the men who own him, and he is good at it. When he stumbles upon the burial rites of the opponent he has just killed, the faith of his ancestors demands a different kind of courage.
6. In the fighting pits of a Carolina plantation, an enslaved man stays alive by killing. But the violence that keeps him breathing threatens to cut him off from the one thing his owners cannot take, the faith of his ancestors.

### Review one — against the corpus's numbers

| # | Words | Cost stated | Ends on | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 37 | yes (body belongs to the owner) | "his soul" — an abstraction | fix the ending |
| 2 | 36 | yes (account for it) | "account for it" — fine | "victim" reframes a forced fight as a crime; change |
| 3 | 45 | yes (which means he kills) | "come looking for him" — good | too long; cut the last clause |
| 4 | 39 | yes (one of them wins) | "faith of their ancestors" — the worn close | "follows the body" is not what happens; he stumbles on it. Fix both |
| 5 | 53 | yes (he is good at it) | "a different kind of courage" — abstraction | far too long; cut sentence two to the fact |
| 6 | 39 | yes (keeps him breathing / cut off) | "faith of his ancestors" — the worn close | the *But* works; change what it lands on |

Three of six ended on the exact family of words the corpus warned about (soul / faith of his ancestors). That is how strong the pull is.

### Review two — against what Luke said he likes and the film's own facts

- "In the heat of a Carolina plantation" is his phrase and better than my "fighting pits of". Use it.
- "One of his own" / "the faith they share" does the work of his "this confrontation with his own people" without saying *people*.
- "He is good at it" is the strongest irony in the set. Keep it, and let it be the whole second half of its sentence.
- **Yoruba** appears nowhere else in the corpus of 168. It is the one word that says *you have not seen this film*. It should be in at least one.
- **Mace**: 62% of shorts name their man. One or two should.
- "Pit" is my image. If the film's fights are not in a pit, every line that says so is wrong. Flagged, not fixed.
- Nothing may end on *freedom, roots, past, soul, faith* alone.

If I had to send one tonight: **5**. It is the plainest, it states the cost in five words ("and he is good at it"), and it ends on a fact. If the film wants its ancestry in the logline, **3**.
