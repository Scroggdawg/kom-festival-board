# Killer of Men title card: studies, 14 October 2026

The frame: Luke's oak-glade still (the two men walking out of the trees), 1920×1080 with the film's letterbox, 2.39 picture in rows 138–942. Supplied as a chat attachment (`source-glade-frame.webp`); the master frame should replace it for finals.

## What the research found (subagent brief, sources below)

Only 12 Years a Slave has a credited type source: real metal Caslon, hand-set and letterpress printed by Typoretum for Vertigo (vertigo.co.uk/12-years-a-slave). The rest is reviews and lookalike sites, marked as such.

- Period dramas set titles in book faces or real printing; westerns and genre films use slabs and wood type (Django Unchained, True Grit, Hostiles).
- Titles over black, held in silence: The Witch, There Will Be Blood. A date or place caption often follows in the title face (There Will Be Blood's "1898").
- American type in 1834: Caslon and Scotch Roman book faces; fat faces for reward notices; Egyptians (slabs) from 1815; wood type from 1828, Leavenworth's pantograph in 1834. The first American sans serif was Boston's "Gothic", 1837, so a sans is close to anachronistic for this film. The 1833 Declaration of the Anti-Slavery Convention: Caslon title, Scotch text, rules between sections (fontsinuse.com/uses/28839).
- Red and black comes from rubrication: red for headings and sacred names, red-and-black title pages 1476–1700s. Django and Mandingo use red for blood and spectacle.

## Principles applied

1. A period book face in capitals (Caslon or Old Standard, the Scotch-style "modern").
2. Small and widely tracked, as Luke asked.
3. One printer's rule under the title: plain, or thick-thin Scotch.
4. Red sparingly, a dark ink red on black; a brighter red only where the type sits over the picture, with the background burned down beneath it.
5. Over black, or in the film's own letterbox, so the picture is not written on.
6. An optional locator in the same face: SOUTH CAROLINA · 1834.
