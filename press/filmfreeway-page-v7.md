## Synopsis

Set in the American South in the 1830s, KILLER OF MEN is a Southern Gothic that follows Mace, an enslaved man forced into underground prizefighting for the profit of his enslaver.

After killing another fighter in the ring, Mace is granted a moment of rest, but accidentally stumbles upon the burial of the man he killed. The chance encounter pulls Mace into a quiet reckoning with guilt, faith, and the violence that sustains his life, revealing the distance between the slave he has been made and the king he was meant to be.

## Director biography

Jordan Betine is a Nigerian-American filmmaker raised between Lagos, Nigeria, and Ashburn, Virginia. He earned his undergraduate degree in film from Howard University. Jordan studied Directing at the American Film Institute, where he directed six short films across genres, including historical drama, social realism, and surrealist comedy. His thesis film, "Killer of Men," is a historical drama set in 1830s South Carolina about an enslaved man who is forced into underground prizefighting. His work explores power, identity, and the African diaspora.

## Director statement

Growing up, I was always one of the youngest in my year, but my height made me one of the tallest in the class. I was often one of the biggest in the room. As I got older, I started noticing how my mere presence was often seen as either a point of praise or persecution. My physicality, tied so closely to my skin, made me seem more menacing, more dangerous than the other boys—even those bigger and stronger than I was. It’s never anything overt, but small, incessant microaggressions. Death by a 1000 needles. It’s a plight that’s shaped me throughout my life, leaving me hyper-aware of myself in spaces. This experience, this reality, is tied to the plight of the Black man in America.

The concept of Mandingo fighting has always fascinated me. It’s a strange, bastardized idea in media and literature. To me, it represents many things: the commercialization of sports and entertainment, the myth of Black-on-Black crime, the portrayal of savagery in history, and the media. It’s always been used to showcase the “Black brute,” but it has never been shown subjectively from the perspective of those involved. What are their lives like? What do they hold dear? What keeps a man going when he’s put in a situation like that?

Setting this film in slavery times, rather than in the present day or during the 1960s, allows me to examine the deep-rooted history of this aggression. Visually, I want the fight scenes to reflect the physical sports Black men often participate in—body slams, boxing, football tackles—to show history repeating itself through various forms of expression.

By setting the story in the 1800s, I also had the chance to explore my heritage. With slavery still in full force and new enslaved people being brought in from Africa, I can examine the past in different ways.

I want to delve into the spirituality of the enslaved at the time, capturing the mix of cultures, beliefs, and religions that slowly formed what we now know as Black culture.

Killer of Men is an expression of pain—absolute brutality and suffering. But it’s also a story of resilience, culture, and spirituality. This story has kept me awake at night, terrified of what I’m putting on the screen.

Killer of Men has been a grueling process—one that’s forced me to question a lot of myself as a man and as a storyteller. This is my Oriki, my folktale, my poem. I won’t shy away from telling difficult stories or giving a voice to the voiceless. I will honor my ancestors and my culture in everything I do.
