# The three that survived — Sep 7, deep pass

*Six writers on separate angles produced 36 loglines. Three reviewers scored all 36 — one on enticement alone, one holding your rulings, one purely adversarial. A reviser built six finals under standing orders not to churn. A final adversary attacked those six and **cut three**. These are the survivors, with its fixes applied.*

---

## A — recommended · 52 words

> In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter survives by killing his own. **Every victory buys him another night and costs a man his life.** He walks in on that man's burial, held in the faith of their shared ancestors, and is forced to reckon with the man he was meant to become.

Opens on the line you called enticing. The crux machine survives, but the cost is no longer an abstraction — it is a man's life, and *that man* is the one whose funeral he walks into two words later. The fight produces the funeral in the same sentence.

*Adversary's fix, applied:* the draft read "costs another man his" — a grammar riddle that made you reach back for "night." Now "costs a man his life."

*Its honest caveat:* this is the safest line in the set, not the most exciting, and it does not carry the surreal ancestral world at all.

## B — the risk · 44 words

> In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter's will to live makes him a killer of his own people. The burial of the man he killed, held in the faith of their shared ancestors, **opens onto a world the men who own him cannot buy.**

The only version that puts the ancestral world on the page as something that *matters to the plantation* rather than sitting beside it. The faith is not comfort here, it is **jurisdiction** — one square foot of that place the owners have no claim on. And "will to live" is conduct, not a gift, so it can damn him.

*Adversary's fixes, applied:* "the man he last killed" → "the man he killed" (removed the most-recent/final ambiguity); "cannot enter" → **"cannot buy"**, which turns a sanctuary into a claim they cannot win, and puts the wagering back in the sentence.

## C — ends on a scene · 58 words

> In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter **wins because losing is dying**, and what he is willing to do to stay alive puts another of his own in the ground. He walks in on that man's burial, held in the faith of their shared ancestors, and **an elder tells him what he would have been in the motherland.**

The only version that says what happens if he loses, and the only one ending on a scene rather than a proposition. An elder naming what he would have been is a thing a programmer wants to watch.

*Adversary's fix, applied:* "He watches that man buried" → "He walks in on that man's burial." *Watching is what the owners do.*

## The best phrase anyone wrote tonight

> **"a world the men who own him cannot enter"** *(now "cannot buy")*

The adversary's note: it is the only phrase all night that promises the thing the strategist said sets your film apart, and it does it in the film's own property logic.

# Three questions only you and Jordan can answer

The adversary's closing finding was that the set cannot get stronger by word-choice alone. Three facts would each do more than any further rewrite.

### 1. Who was the man he killed?

All six versions call him a category — "his own," "that man," "the last body." *Son of Saul* works because the corpse is **"a boy he takes for his son."** One true fact — a name, a field they worked together, that Mace knows the song they are singing over him — would do more than every word change on this page.

### 2. One concrete noun from inside the surreal sequence.

B gets to the threshold, C gets the elder, but no line names a single thing he **sees** on the other side, because nobody has told me one. Give me one image from that world and the whole set goes up a level.

### 3. Does he fight again after the burial?

No version has a forward beat, because the facts don't establish one. If he does fight again, that is the fuse the set is missing, and it is one clause.
