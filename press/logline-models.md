# Logline models — the short version

*The readable companion to `research/reckoning-loglines.md` (119 entries, a reference to search, not to read). Examples only. Every line verbatim from a published source.*

---

## The six constructions, in plain English

### 1. The machine — one trait, credited and charged

**One quality is named, given the win, and then billed for the ruin. Same quality, both times.**

> **Cool Hand Luke.** "Luke's bravado… make him a rebel hero to his fellow convicts **and** a thorn in the side of the prison officers."

One verb — *make* — two opposite results. The bravado isn't fighting him; it's working, and the working is the damage.

> **Champion** (1949). "An **unscrupulous** boxer fights his way to the top, but eventually alienates all of the people who helped him on the way up."

One adjective does everything. And notice the cost is *people*, not a feeling.

> **American Sniper.** "His pinpoint accuracy **not only** saves countless lives **but also** makes him a prime target of insurgents."

The trait is the subject of the sentence. Two verbs hang off it, pointing opposite ways.

**This is your construction.** You already have it.

---

### 2. The mask becomes the face

**A role taken on to survive turns into who he actually is.**

> **Black Book.** "To fight the enemy, she must become one of them." *(ten words)*
>
> **Cell 211.** "To survive inside, he has to become one of them." *(ten words)*
>
> **Europa Europa.** "A Jewish boy… **poses as** a German orphan and is taken into the heart of the Nazi world as a 'war hero' and **eventually becomes** a Hitler Youth."

Watch the verb change in that last one: *poses as* → *becomes*. One adverb, "eventually," does the whole character arc. No interiority claimed, no "must confront" — just a different verb.

> **Donnie Brasco.** "An FBI undercover agent infiltrates the mob and identifies more with the mafia life **at the expense of** his regular one."

You flagged "at the expense of." It's doing what a "but" would do, for three words, and "his regular one" shrinks an entire marriage into an afterthought — which is exactly what it became.

---

### 3. The price, itemised

**Success or survival is stated, then what it cost is named as a specific thing.**

> **The Grey Zone.** "…assisting in the extermination of fellow Jews **in exchange for a few more months of life.**"

An actual exchange rate. And the payment is quantified *small*, so you do the arithmetic yourself and find it monstrous.

> **Kapò.** "Determined to survive at any price… manages to survive **by** accepting the role of kapo, a privileged prisoner whose mission is to ruthlessly guard other prisoners."

The whole moral weight sits on one preposition: *by*. Survives **by** doing the unforgivable thing.

---

### 4. The choice — two selves, one continues

**He must decide which version of himself goes on.** The smallest group in the corpus, and the one that fights the machine: if you've already said the violence is costing him his humanity, you've answered the question before you ask it.

> **City of God.** "…two young men **choose different paths.** Rocket is a budding photographer… **while** Li'l Zé is an ambitious drug dealer…"

Same slum, same pressure, two answers, one sentence.

---

### 5. The interior given a physical arena

**The inside fight is handed a place to happen.**

> **Hunger.** "An odyssey, in which the smallest gestures become epic and when **the body is the last resource** for protest."

When everything else has been taken, the self is the only material left — so destroying it is the only way to use it.

---

### 6. An event forces him to see himself

**Something happens; he has to look.** This is the shape your film actually has — the burial is the event.

## Your genre — coerced survival

The strongest lines in the whole corpus came from here: men and women who did terrible things to stay alive, under someone else's power. Every one of these is your cousin.

> **Son of Saul.** "In the horror of 1944 Auschwitz, a prisoner forced to burn the corpses of his own people finds **moral survival** trying to save from the flames the body of a boy he takes for his son, seeking to give him a proper Jewish burial."

**Read that twice.** A man forced to handle the dead of his own people, who finds his soul by getting one body a proper burial. That is Killer of Men's exact shape, and the phrase "moral survival" is the move — it concedes that staying alive is already secured and already worthless, and relocates the stakes.

> **A Prophet.** "…Cornered by the leader of the Corsican gang… **he is given** a number of 'missions' to carry out, toughening him up… Malik **is** a fast learner and **rises**…"

The grammar performs the takeover: passive while he's a victim, active once he's become the thing.

> **Beasts of No Nation.** "Child. Captive. Killer." *(three words)*

No verbs. The full stops are elapsed time, and you do the causation yourself. The middle word is load-bearing — without *Captive* it's an accusation; with it, it indicts someone offscreen.

## The trait slot — what goes where "the violence" is

The finding, plainly: **the reader has to want the trait before the sentence takes it away.** Bravado, accuracy, perfectionism — you'd take those. That's why the sentence hurts when it turns.

| Option | Note |
|---|---|
| the speed of his hands | Most concrete and most enviable. *Golden Boy* built a film on a fighter's hands |
| his strength | Plainest — and it is what his owner values him for, so the economics are built in |
| the ferocity that has kept him alive | One degree more animal than yours |
| the deadly skill that has kept him alive | Yours. "Skill" makes it a craft, which is more enviable than "violence" |
| the gift that has kept him alive | Ironic — a gift he never asked for. Riskiest |
| the violence that has kept him alive | Locked. Honest, least enviable |

## Where the line stands

> In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter. With every victory, the violence that has kept him alive costs him a little more of his humanity. When he stumbles upon his opponent's burial ceremony, held in the faith of their shared ancestors, he is forced to reckon with the life that was taken.

**52 words.** For scale: *Night Feeds'* own logline is 43 words in three sentences, and it ends on a question.

The live fragment that may beat it:

> With every life he takes — his own — he slips deeper into the grasp of his oppressors.

That names the oppressors as the beneficiaries of his skill, which is the thing this film is about.
