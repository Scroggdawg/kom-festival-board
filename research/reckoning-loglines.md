# Loglines of the Raging Bull kind — a sourced corpus

*Compiled September 7, 2026. Six worlds searched: the fight film, the double life, ambition, war and conscience, survival under domination, appetite. **Found, not written** — every line verbatim from a published source with its URL. 119 entries survived; 24 were dropped by verifiers for failing the construction test, which is the point: a corpus padded with near-misses would be worthless.*

## What was being hunted

Not "man versus himself." **One named trait, credited with the victory and charged with the ruin, in the same breath.** The reader can run the machine forward: whatever makes him win will make him lose.

# The purest seven

SEVEN entries that most purely execute the machine — one named trait credited with the victory and charged with the ruin in the same breath.

1. AMERICAN SNIPER (2014) — the purest thing in the corpus.
QUOTE: "U.S. Navy SEAL Chris Kyle takes his sole mission—protect his comrades—to heart and becomes one of the most lethal snipers in American history. His pinpoint accuracy not only saves countless lives but also makes him a prime target of insurgents. Despite grave danger and his struggle to be a good husband and father to his family back in the States, Kyle serves four tours of duty in Iraq. However, when he finally returns home, he finds that he cannot leave the war behind."
TRAIT: pinpoint accuracy. COST: makes him a prime target of insurgents; he cannot leave the war behind.
GRAMMAR: the trait is the grammatical SUBJECT, and the correlative "not only X but also Y" hangs two predicates off it. One noun, two verbs, opposite valences, no adversative between the man and himself. Only "not only... but also" in 119 entries, and the tightest yoke available in English: 17 words for the whole machine.

2. COOL HAND LUKE (1967)
QUOTE: "Luke's bravado, even in the face of repeated stints in the prison's dreaded solitary confinement cell, "the box," make him a rebel hero to his fellow convicts and a thorn in the side of the prison officers."
TRAIT: Luke's bravado / his unbreakable will. COST: a thorn in the side of the prison officers.
GRAMMAR: one subject, ONE verb ("make"), two object complements joined by "and". Victory and ruin are grammatically the same act — the sentence cannot separate them, because they share the verb. Cheapest construction available: the "and" costs one word.

3. CHAMPION (1949)
QUOTE: "An unscrupulous boxer fights his way to the top, but eventually alienates all of the people who helped him on the way up."
TRAIT: unscrupulous. COST: alienates all of the people who helped him on the way up.
GRAMMAR: a single-word adjectival trait fronted into the subject, then two coordinated predicates split by "but". The whole machine costs 23 words, one of which carries the character. Also the corpus's best example of naming the cost as PEOPLE rather than an abstraction.

4. WHIPLASH (2014, official)
QUOTE: "Andrew's passion to achieve perfection quickly spirals into obsession, as his ruthless teacher continues to push him to the brink of both his ability—and his sanity."
TRAIT: Andrew's passion to achieve perfection. COST: the brink of both his ability—and his sanity.
GRAMMAR: two moves. The trait is the subject of its own mutation ("passion... spirals into obsession" — the thing changes state with no person acting on it). Then "the brink of BOTH his ability—AND his sanity" forces win and ruin into one prepositional object, with an em dash holding the seam open so the second item lands.

5. KAPÒ (1960) — closest premise-match to KOM in the whole corpus.
QUOTE: "Determined to survive at any price, Edith, a young Jewish woman deported to an extermination camp, manages to survive by accepting the role of kapo, a privileged prisoner whose mission is to ruthlessly guard other prisoners."
TRAIT: Determined to survive at any price. COST: accepting the role of kapo... whose mission is to ruthlessly guard other prisoners.
GRAMMAR: fronted participial trait, then "manages to survive BY [the atrocity]" — the preposition "by" does the entire moral work, naming the means of victory and the substance of ruin as one thing. An appositive plus a "whose" relative then loads the horror onto the noun without a new clause.

6. FOXCATCHER (2014, official)
QUOTE: "Based on actual events, FOXCATCHER is a gripping and profoundly American story of fragile men who pinned their hopes for love and redemption on a desperate obsession for greatness that was to end in tragedy."
TRAIT: a desperate obsession for greatness. COST: that was to end in tragedy.
GRAMMAR: the relative clause. "a desperate obsession for greatness THAT WAS TO END IN TRAGEDY" — 11 words that name the driver and sentence it inside one noun phrase. Spends ZERO extra clauses on the win, because the win is packed into the noun. Most word-efficient version of the machine in the corpus, and the one KOM's locked cost clause already imitates.

7. SERPICO (1973)
QUOTE: "Many of his fellow officers considered him the most dangerous man alive - an honest cop."
TRAIT: an honest cop. COST: the most dangerous man alive.
GRAMMAR: inverted. The consequence is stated first as an apparent slur; the trait is withheld to the final position behind a dash, where it detonates as the explanation. 15 words. Its twin is The French Connection (1971): "Doyle is bad news—but a good cop." — 8 words, same dash, same reversal, with "but" pivoting inside a single clause.

RUNNERS-UP (8-10): Days of Wine and Roses ("...a young woman, whom he systematically addicts to booze so they can share his "passion" together." — the trait is the bond AND the poison); Flight ("...has a problem with drugs and alcohol, though so far he's managed to complete his flights safely"); The French Connection.

# The grammar, counted

Devices counted by hand over the 99 entries carrying a construction (119 total minus the 20 classified "none"). Borderline calls exist; made consistently, stated as hand-classified rather than machine-extracted.

YOKE WORDS
- "But" or "However" as the hinge: 15/99 (15%). Fight 4 (Champion, Golden Boy, The Wrestler, Southpaw); double-life 3 (The Lives of Others, The Americans, The French Connection); ambition 2 (Uncut Gems, The Wrestler); war 2 (American Sniper x2); survival 0; appetite 4 (Flight, Shame, Scarface, The Wolf of Wall Street).
  CRITICAL: in all 15, the "but" sits between the two EFFECTS, never between the man and himself. Not one entry in 119 puts an adversative between a character and his own interior.
- Any other explicit reversal hinge (although/despite/while/until/when/as/even as/though): 16/99. Combined with "but": 31/99 (31%) use an explicit hinge word at all. The other 69% yoke win and ruin with grammar alone.
- "not only... but also": 1/99. Only American Sniper. Rarest and purest.

RELATIVE CLAUSE ("the X that/who/whose/which Y")
- 15/99 (15%): Foxcatcher, Better Call Saul, White Boy Rick, Training Day, The Lives of Others, American Sniper, The Act of Killing, Kapò, Days of Wine and Roses, The Deer Hunter, Sound of Metal, Son of Saul, Infernal Affairs, The Immortal, Champion ("all of the people WHO helped him on the way up").
- But 4 of the purest 7 use it. Under-used relative to how well it works, and the only device that costs nothing per extra idea, because it loads the fate onto a noun already in the sentence.

"AND" AS THE YOKE (one subject or one verb taking two opposed consequences)
- 17/99 (17%): Cool Hand Luke, American Sniper x2, Platoon ("fights to survive the battlefield and the war within his unit" — one verb, one external object, one internal), Winter Light ("fails a loving woman, a suicidal fisherman and God"), Europa Europa, A Prophet, White Boy Rick x2, The Act of Killing, Beautiful Boxer, Uncut Gems, Whiplash, Southpaw, Candy, Jawbone, Sound of Metal.

PUNCTUATION
- Em dash or hyphen-dash: 10/99 (Serpico, The French Connection, White Boy Rick, Whiplash x2, Rush, Half Nelson, The Deer Hunter, The Grey Zone, Infernal Affairs).
- Colon: 2/99 (Land of Mine, The Assignment).
- SEMICOLON: 0/119. Not one entry in the entire corpus uses a semicolon. The em dash has completely displaced it in this register.

HOW THE COST IS NAMED
- As a person or concrete object: ~20/99 (20%). Champion "all of the people who helped him on the way up"; Southpaw "his family, his house and his manager"; Winter Light "a loving woman, a suicidal fisherman and God"; The Wrestler "a long-in-the-tooth stripper... his estranged daughter"; Traffic "his well-educated daughter"; Girlfight "her father"; Kapò "other prisoners"; The Grey Zone "fellow Jews" / "a few more months of life"; Son of Saul "the corpses of his own people"; Grizzly Man "the last thirteen years of his life"; Days of Wine and Roses "a young woman"; The Americans "his relationship with Elizabeth"; Bleed for This "a broken neck".
- As an abstract noun that is nonetheless specific and ownable (humanity, sanity, faith, innocence, equilibrium, his own personality): ~24/99 (24%).
- As an event or outcome (arrest, downfall, spiral, death, prison, relapse): ~55/99 (55%).
- The 20 concrete ones are disproportionately the memorable ones. The 20 rejected "none" entries fill the same slot with an unownable abstraction ("demons", "his past").

TRAIT POSITION (measured over the 19 trait_two_consequences entries, where the question is answerable)
- Trait named BEFORE the victory: 14/19 (74%). Champion, American Sniper x2, Cool Hand Luke, Whiplash, Foxcatcher, Jesse James, Flight, Requiem for a Dream, Another Round, Candy, Days of Wine and Roses, The Wrestler, Mesrine.
- Victory stated first, trait named AFTERWARD as the cause of ruin: 5/19 (26%). Bleed for This, Serpico, The French Connection, Uncut Gems, Scarface.
- 3 of those 5 are taglines. The withheld-trait reveal is the more memorable form and the less usable one: it needs a reader who already knows the film.

LENGTH
- The machine, executed once, costs 11-23 words: Foxcatcher 11 (inside a longer sentence), American Sniper 17 (one sentence), Champion 23 (complete logline).
- Every corpus specimen under 16 words is a TAGLINE, not a logline: Beasts of No Nation 3, Ozark 5, Shot Caller 5, Platoon 7, A Hidden Life 8, The French Connection 8, Cell 211 9, Black Book 9, Serpico 15.
- Official festival/distributor synopses here run 60-110 words (Whiplash 88, Infernal Affairs 90, Uncut Gems 65, Kapò 35).

# For Killer of Men

CONSTRUCTION: trait_two_consequences, and he already has it. Do not switch.
DEVICE: the relative clause inside the subject noun phrase, plus an em dash for the turn. Not "but" (15/99, and it costs a whole clause). Not a semicolon (0/119 — nobody in this register uses one).

WHY THAT DEVICE. His locked cost clause — "the violence that has kept him alive costs him a little more of his humanity with every victory" — is already Foxcatcher's grammar: "a desperate obsession for greatness THAT WAS TO END IN TRAGEDY". The relative clause is the only device in the corpus that costs nothing per extra idea, because it credits the trait with the victory ("that has kept him alive") without spending a separate clause on the victory. That lock is the strongest thing he has and should be the last thing he cuts.

WORD ARITHMETIC — the honest number is worse than he thinks. His locks are not ~30 words. Counted exactly:
  "In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter" ............................... 7
  "the violence that has kept him alive costs him a little more of his humanity with every victory" ... 18
  "held in the faith of their shared ancestors" ................................... 8
  "live as a killer or stand up for the life that was taken from him" ............. 15
  LOCKED TOTAL ................................................................... 48

Connective tissue cannot be under ~12-16 words (a verb for the killing, a verb for the stumbling-upon, a "must choose whether to"). All four locks verbatim floors him at 60-64 words. Under 50 is arithmetically impossible with all four. That is the finding; give it to him as arithmetic, not opinion.

WHICH LOCK TO RELEASE. The ending. Evidence: the_choice is the smallest working class (11/119, 9%) and the one construction that structurally COMPETES with the machine. You cannot in one sentence say the violence has already been costing him his humanity with every victory AND that he has yet to decide who he is — the first proposition has answered the second. Not one of the 19 trait_two_consequences entries also carries a choice clause. Zero of the purest seven do. Releasing the ending buys 15 words, and the cost clause already implies the stake.

The arithmetic then works. Three locks verbatim, twelve connective words, one em dash carrying the turn:
  "In 1834 South Carolina, an enslaved prizefighter kills for the men who own him, and the violence that has kept him alive costs him a little more of his humanity with every victory — until he stumbles on his opponent's burial, held in the faith of their shared ancestors."
  = 48 words. Under target, with every lock he actually needs intact.
(That is an ARRANGEMENT of his own locked words plus twelve connectives, offered as budget proof — not a logline proposed over his.)

If he insists on all four locks: accept 60-64 and stop apologising for it. The corpus's official festival and distributor synopses run 60-110 words. Under-50 here is tagline territory, not logline territory. The 50-word rule is a competition-form convention, not a craft law, and the evidence does not support mutilating the machine to meet it.

THREE CHEAP CUTS IF HE WANTS THEM ANYWAY:
- "for the men who own him" is redundant with "enslaved" — Champion spends exactly one word on character ("unscrupulous"). Saves 6.
- Em dash plus "until" is the corpus's cheapest reversal (Rush 2013, Half Nelson, Fight Club): 2 words for a hinge a "but" clause would charge 8 for.
- "has kept" -> "keeps" saves 1 without touching the shape of the lock.

ONE WARNING. "a little more of his humanity" is a hedge. The corpus does not hedge costs: "even his humanity" (Whiplash), "at any price" (Kapò), "in exchange for a few more months of life" (The Grey Zone). Twenty entries name the cost as a person or object; the abstraction-plus-quantifier form appears nowhere in the purest seven. He locked it, so it stays — but he should know "a little more of" is the one place his sentence lets the machine slip, and the corpus's answer to that slot is a named person, not a fraction.

# Which worlds produce this language

WHICH LANES ACTUALLY PRODUCED THE PUREST SPECIMENS. Distribution of the purest seven: war-conscience 1 (American Sniper), survival 2 (Cool Hand Luke, Kapò), ambition 2 (Whiplash, Foxcatcher), fight 1 (Champion), double-life 1 (Serpico). Appetite produced ZERO of the purest seven despite contributing the most trait_two_consequences entries in the corpus (6 of 19).

That is the finding worth carrying. The machine works when the trait is a SKILL OR A VIRTUE — accuracy, bravado, honesty, perfectionism, determination to live. It degrades when the trait is a VICE. Appetite loglines default to price_named and to "spiral" because a vice does not need to be credited with a victory: the reader already knows drinking is bad, so the sentence has no irony to spend. Kyle's accuracy and Luke's bravado are things the reader would want to have. That gap between what the reader admires and what it costs IS the machine.

KOM's trait — violence as a survival skill — sits in the war/fight/survival family, on the virtue side. He is in the right neighbourhood.

FOUR DOMAINS NOT SEARCHED HERE WHERE THE CONSTRUCTION SHOULD BE COMMON:

1. MEDICINE, especially surgery and battlefield triage. The detachment that lets a surgeon cut is a professional virtue with a named human cost, and the copy tends to say so out loud because hospital drama sells on exactly that irony. Expect a high hit rate on relative-clause forms ("the steadiness that saves strangers"). The corpus has nothing from this world; it should be the first follow-up lane.

2. COERCED COLLABORATION UNDER SLAVERY AND OCCUPATION — slave drivers, gladiators, kapos, Sonderkommando, sepoys, informants, camp trusties. This is KOM's actual genre and the corpus is thin here: only Kapò, The Grey Zone, Son of Saul, Europa Europa, Cell 211. Two of those are among the strongest specimens in 119 entries, which implies a very high yield per search. Recommend a dedicated lane. Targets: 12 Years a Slave, Django Unchained, Nightjohn, The Birth of a Nation (2016), Spartacus, Gladiator, Sankofa, Emperor, judicial-combat framings, and the Brazilian/Caribbean maroon films.

3. LAW — prosecution, capital defence, advocacy generally. "The zeal that wins the case" is a named professional trait with an itemisable body count, and legal-thriller copy has a century of practice saying it in one sentence. Expect heavy use of the "and" double-predicate.

4. ATHLETICS AND PERFORMANCE OUTSIDE COMBAT — ballet, gymnastics, endurance sport, classical music beyond Whiplash. Trait is pain tolerance or perfectionism; cost is a body or a childhood. Ambition produced 2 of the purest 7 from only 16 entries, the best per-entry rate of any lane, and this is its unexplored half.

TWO FURTHER CANDIDATES IF BUDGET ALLOWS: war photography and front-line journalism (the nerve that gets the picture is the nerve that stops him putting the camera down — Grizzly Man is the corpus's only specimen from this family and it is a good one); and caregiving/nursing/social work (the empathy that makes her indispensable is what burns her out — likely the purest virtue-cost pairing available, and completely absent here).

# Is it "man versus himself"? — the evidence

HE IS PARTLY RIGHT AND THE FRAMING WILL STILL MISLEAD HIM. Both, plainly.

WHERE HE IS RIGHT. "More than man versus man" is correct and the corpus proves it. Every entry whose logline organises itself around an external opponent fell to "none": Body and Soul (shady characters tempt him), Carlito's Way (sucked back into the underworld), Casino (until Ginger gets between them), Ford v Ferrari (corporate interference, the laws of physics). Man-vs-man copy produces plot summary. He is also right that the price must be paid IN the man rather than in the plot: the 55/99 entries naming the cost as an event (arrest, downfall, spiral) are consistently weaker than the 20 naming it as a person he loses or the 24 naming it as something ownable he spends.

WHERE THE FRAMING IS WRONG. "Man versus himself" is not a description of the machine — it is the name of the failure mode. The clearest evidence is inside his own corpus. Twenty entries were classified "none", and the loglines that most explicitly announce man-vs-himself are among them: Ford v Ferrari's "their own personal demons", A Star Is Born (2018)'s "his own internal demons", Waltz with Bashir's "One man's journey to uncover his past". Textbook internal conflict, stated as such, and the worst copy in the collection. If he goes looking for man-vs-himself, that is what he will find and that is what he will write.

WHAT HE HAS ACTUALLY IDENTIFIED IS CAUSAL IRONY, NOT CONFLICT. Reread the specimen: nothing in the Raging Bull line depicts LaMotta struggling against himself. The rage does not fight him. It works. It wins him the championship, and the winning is the damage. One cause, two opposite valences, stated without hesitation on either side.

The grammar proves it, and this is the part he should take most seriously. In all 15 entries that use "but" or "however" as the hinge, the adversative sits between the two EFFECTS — never between the man and himself. Not one entry in 119 places an adversative between a character and his own interior. Cool Hand Luke's bravado does not war with Luke; it makes him a hero AND a thorn in a single verb. American Sniper's accuracy does not war with Kyle; it saves lives AND paints a target. The conflict, where there is one, is between the READER'S two readings of one fact. That is a rhetorical event, not a psychological one.

THREE WAYS THE FRAMING WOULD COST HIM CONCRETELY.
1. It invites him to write hesitation, doubt and conscience — struggle-language — which is precisely the abstraction the corpus rejects. Interiority in these loglines is never depicted; it is CHARGED, as a bill.
2. It pushes him toward the_choice construction: smallest working class (11/119) and the one that cancels the machine. If the trait is already costing him his humanity with every victory, the question of who he is has been answered before the choice is offered. Zero of the 19 trait_two_consequences entries also carry a choice clause.
3. It has already cost him words. His locked ending is a man-vs-himself proposition bolted onto a causal-irony middle, and that seam is exactly where his 48 locked words overrun 50.

ONE COMPLICATION HE SHOULD KNOW ABOUT. His premise resists the machine slightly, and he should not be surprised when it fights him. The pure machine credits a trait the man CHOSE and owns — Kyle's accuracy, Luke's bravado, Andrew's passion. KOM's fighter did not choose to be violent; it was compelled. That is why the corpus's two closest premise-matches, Kapò and The Grey Zone, both reach for price_named ("at any price", "in exchange for a few more months of life") rather than trait_two_consequences: a coerced trait cannot be credited with a victory in the same proud way. His locked phrasing solves this, and he should know it solved it — "the violence that has kept him alive" credits the violence with SURVIVAL rather than with glory. That is the coerced man's version of the machine, and it is the correct one for his film.

VERDICT: keep the irony, drop the vocabulary. Stop calling it man versus himself and start calling it what it is — the same fact, credited and charged. If the ending must stay, make the choice the consequence of the irony rather than a second proposition standing beside it.

# The corpus, by construction

## The machine — one trait, credited and charged (19)

**The French Connection** (1971) · 8w · database

> Doyle is bad news—but a good cop.

*Trait:* a good cop · *Cost:* Doyle is bad news

Eight words, one em dash, and the same man is named twice — once as the damage and once as the virtue. The 'but' does not soften the charge, it fuses the two readings so neither can be discarded. This is the Raging Bull machine at the smallest possible scale. [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/the-french-connection/)

**Serpico** (1973) · 15w · database

> Many of his fellow officers considered him the most dangerous man alive - an honest cop.

*Trait:* an honest cop · *Cost:* the most dangerous man alive

Withholds the trait until after the dash, so the sentence detonates backwards. 'The most dangerous man alive' loads the reader with an expectation of villainy; 'an honest cop' collapses it and re-aims the danger at the institution. The single virtue is both his identity and the charge against him. [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/serpico/)

**Requiem for a Dream** (2000) · 15w · database

> The drug-induced utopias of four Coney Island residents are shattered when their addictions run deep.

*Trait:* drug-induced utopias · *Cost:* are shattered when their addictions run deep

Extreme compression of the specimen. One noun phrase — "drug-induced utopias" — carries the gift, and one verb — "shattered" — carries the bill, and the causal agent of both is the same word ("drug" / "addictions"). Fifteen words and nothing is spent on plot. [source](https://www.themoviedb.org/movie/641-requiem-for-a-dream)

**Champion** (1949) · 23w · database

> An unscrupulous boxer fights his way to the top, but eventually alienates all of the people who helped him on the way up.

*Trait:* unscrupulous · *Cost:* alienates all of the people who helped him on the way up

The purest compression of the specimen in the whole lane. One adjective — 'unscrupulous' — is both the engine of the rise and the invoice for it, and 'but eventually' is the entire machine: not a reversal, just the passage of time letting the same quality finish its work. Note the second clause does not name a new flaw; it names the *people*, so the cost is populated rather than abstract. [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/champion/)

**Days of Wine and Roses** (1962) · 26w · database

> An alcoholic falls in love with and gets married to a young woman, whom he systematically addicts to booze so they can share his "passion" together.

*Trait:* his "passion" · *Cost:* whom he systematically addicts to booze

The appetite is what he has to give her, so the gift and the injury are one act. The sentence indicts itself with punctuation — scare quotes around "passion" — and stages a collision of registers in a single clause: the romantic verb ("share") sitting next to the clinical one ("systematically addicts"). "So they can" is the killer: it supplies his sincere motive for the damage. [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/days-of-wine-and-roses/)

**Foxcatcher** (2014) · 35w · official

> Based on actual events, FOXCATCHER is a gripping and profoundly American story of fragile men who pinned their hopes for love and redemption on a desperate obsession for greatness that was to end in tragedy.

*Trait:* a desperate obsession for greatness · *Cost:* that was to end in tragedy

The relative clause is doing the fork: "a desperate obsession for greatness THAT WAS TO END IN TRAGEDY" welds the drive and the outcome into one grammatical unit, so the reader cannot hold the ambition in mind without the ending attached. Also note "pinned their hopes for love and redemption ON" — it makes the obsession a load-bearing object, something they stand on, which is why the collapse is structural rather than moral. [source](https://athenacinema.com/foxcatcher/)

**American Sniper** (2014) · 43w · database

> Navy SEAL sniper Chris Kyle's pinpoint accuracy saves countless lives on the battlefield and turns him into a legend. Back home to his wife and kids after four tours of duty, however, Chris finds that it is the war he can't leave behind.

*Trait:* pinpoint accuracy · *Cost:* Chris finds that it is the war he can't leave behind

Names one measurable skill, credits it with the victory in clause one, then lets the same skill be the reason he cannot come home. The pivot word is 'however' and the sting is the inversion in the last clause — not 'he can't leave the war behind' but 'it is the war he can't leave behind,' which makes the war the agent and him the object. [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/american-sniper/)

**Candy** (2006) · 43w · database

> A poet falls in love with an art student, who gravitates to his bohemian lifestyle — and his love of heroin. Hooked as much on one another as they are on the drug, their relationship alternates between states of oblivion, self-destruction, and despair.

*Trait:* Hooked as much on one another as they are on the drug · *Cost:* their relationship alternates between states of oblivion, self-destruction, and despair

Fuses the good thing and the fatal thing into one grammatical object so neither can be removed without the other — "as much on one another as they are on the drug" makes the love and the habit the same appetite. The em-dash in "his bohemian lifestyle — and his love of heroin" does the same trick a beat earlier: the charm and the disease arrive as one offer. [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/candy-2006/)

**Uncut Gems** (2019) · 47w · database

> Howard is a charismatic, scheming diamond dealer who intends to sell an Ethiopian rock full of embedded gems at auction. But for this he will need to navigate his way through marital problems, girlfriend problems, imaginatively vicious racketeers, and his unquenchable desire to be close to success.

*Trait:* his unquenchable desire to be close to success · *Cost:* marital problems, girlfriend problems, imaginatively vicious racketeers

The list is the whole trick, and specifically the ORDER of the list. Three external obstacles — wife, girlfriend, racketeers — and then, in the same grammatical slot, "his unquenchable desire to be close to success." By making the appetite the fourth item in a list of enemies, the sentence rules that his drive is an obstacle of the same kind and severity as the men who want to hurt him. That is the specimen's claim delivered by syntax alone, with no explanatory clause at all. [source](https://mubi.com/en/us/films/uncut-gems)

**Mesrine: Public Enemy #1** (2008) · 50w · database

> After nearly two decades of legendary criminal feats, making him France's most notorious criminal while simultaneously feeding his desire for media attention and public adoration, Mesrine becomes increasingly paranoid and isolated, leading to a dramatic confrontation with the law that ultimately seals his fate as the nation's most infamous public enemy.

*Trait:* his desire for media attention and public adoration · *Cost:* Mesrine becomes increasingly paranoid and isolated

'While simultaneously' is the hinge word — it forces the credit and the charge into the same clause instead of letting them sequence. The appetite for adoration builds the legend and hollows the man at the same instant, and the sentence refuses to separate the two. [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/mesrine-public-enemy-no-1/)

**The Assassination of Jesse James by the Coward Robert Ford** (2007) · 51w · database

> Outlaw Jesse James is rumored to be the 'fastest gun in the West'. An eager recruit into James' notorious gang, Robert Ford eventually grows jealous of the famed outlaw and, when Robert and his brother sense an opportunity to kill James, their murderous action elevates their target to near mythical status.

*Trait:* An eager recruit into James' notorious gang, Robert Ford eventually grows jealous of the famed outlaw · *Cost:* their murderous action elevates their target to near mythical status

The trait is Ford's worship, and it is credited with both his admission to the gang ('an eager recruit') and his murder of the man he worshipped ('grows jealous'). Then the final clause turns the machine one more notch: the killing does the opposite of what killing is for — it 'elevates their target,' so Ford destroys the man and manufactures the god. [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/the-assassination-of-jesse-james-by-the-coward-robert-ford/)

**The Wrestler** (2008) · 71w · official

> Aging wrestler Randy "The Ram" Robinson (Mickey Rourke) is long past his prime but still ready and rarin' to go on the pro-wrestling circuit. ## Negative examples — why these fail (20)

**Bad Lieutenant** (1992) · 5w · database

> Gambler. Thief. Junkie. Killer. Cop.

*Trait:* Gambler. Thief. Junkie. Killer. · *Cost:* Cop.

Word order is the entire construction. Five nouns, four full stops, and the office arrives LAST — by the time the reader reaches "Cop" the appetites have already taken the man, so the badge reads as the vestige rather than the identity. Inverts the usual mask logic: the role was the mask, and the appetites are the face that ate it. [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/bad-lieutenant/)

**Wall Street** (1987) · 5w · database

> Every dream has a price.

*Trait:* Every dream · *Cost:* has a price

The compressed limit case, and instructive in both directions: five words state the lane's entire thesis, but nothing concrete is named, so it works as a poster and would fail as a logline. Shows exactly where the price_named construction stops being useful — the exchange is asserted, the currency is not. [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/wall-street/)

**The Sisters Brothers** (2018) · 6w · database

*Trait:* Make a killing · *Cost:* n/a — the pun fuses the skill and its wage without naming a price

Two identical constructions where only the final noun changes, so the pun forces 'killing' and 'living' into the same grammatical slot and therefore the same moral one. Six words, one verb repeated, and the trade is complete. [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/the-sisters-brothers/)

**Waltz with Bashir** (2008) · 7w · database

None — recorded as a negative specimen. 'Journey' plus 'uncover his past' is the exact failure mode: two abstractions that could be attached to several thousand films and predict nothing about any of them. [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/waltz-with-bashir/)

**Hacksaw Ridge** (2016) · 12w · database

A single sentence that sets up a category and then violates its defining condition.
