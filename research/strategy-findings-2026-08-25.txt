
==================== PUBLICISTS ====================

### Q1 — Do shorts hire publicists, and when is it worth it?
SUMMARY: Most shorts do NOT hire a publicist — even Sundance's own in-house publicist says short filmmakers usually rely on the festival's press office, which pitches press on the film's behalf. Hiring one becomes rational only in two situations: a world premiere at a top-tier festival (Sundance/SXSW/Tribeca) where the director has a concrete career objective (representation, feature financing), or a later Oscar-qualification/awards campaign. Never hire before you have an acceptance in hand.
DETAIL: Sundance publicist Susan Szotyori (No Film School Q&A): 'For short films, filmmakers oftentimes opt not to hire a publicist' — the festival's in-house publicity team then pitches press, connects filmmakers with journalists, and handles interview logistics; she calls DIY campaigns 'often successful' for shorts, while recommending publicists for features. The Film Collaborative's festival-PR guide is blunt: 'Don't hire a publicist if you haven't gotten into a film festival or have a theatrical/digital release' — you get no return if the film isn't screening. K2 Publicity (via No Film School) frames the trigger as 'world premiering at a major or specialty festival.' For a 13-minute AFI thesis short, the practical read (inference, consistent across sources): skip paid PR for regional/mid-tier premieres; consider it only for a Sundance/SXSW/Tribeca premiere or an Oscar-shortlist push, and treat the director-as-emerging-talent story (Nigerian director, Yoruba spiritual drama, AFI thesis) as the pitchable angle, since publicists say angle-finding is their core value (Film Festival Secrets / Bright Iris).

### Q2 — Real cost numbers: festival PR packages and retainers
SUMMARY: Festival publicity is usually flat-fee, not retainer: roughly $7K–12K for a major festival (Sundance/TIFF/Cannes/SXSW) and $3K–7K for regional or locally-based festivals (LA, AFI FEST, New York, Tribeca), per The Film Collaborative. Boutique indie-film publicists start around $5K per campaign; monthly retainers run $3K–20K at the extremes. Shorts-specific awards campaigns run from a few thousand dollars (Academy mailshots) to $5K–10K typical, up to $100K+ comprehensive; a full Oscar-shorts push with top firms implies $10K–15K/month PR retainers.
DETAIL: The Film Collaborative (festival PR budgeting): major festivals '$7-12K' because publicists cover their own travel/lodging; regional festivals '$3-7K' using locally-based publicists; festival campaigns are flat-fee. AMW's film-PR pricing guide: boutique indie specialists start ~$5,000; monthly retainers $3K–$20K (top end is celebrity publicists); notes most film publicists charge project fees, and typical indie marketing spend overall is $2,500–$7,500. For shorts specifically, Screen Daily's shorts-campaign feature (quoting London Flair's Catherine Lyn Scott and See-Through Films' Kathleen McInnis): minimal campaigns are 'a few thousand dollars' for Academy mailshots, typical $5,000–$10,000, comprehensive up to $100,000. The Rough Cut newsletter's Oscar-shorts breakdown puts a full modern campaign at $150K–$300K: PR retainers $60K–90K over ~6 months, Academy Screening Room fees $12.5K–20K, trade ads ~$50K, voter events $20K–50K, with success bonuses ($15K–25K for a nomination, $20K–50K for a win) — and notes no individual filmmakers self-fund at that scale (funders are streamers, boutique distributors, or EP-credit buyers). Caveat: the $3K–12K festival figures were written for features; no published shorts-specific festival flat fee surfaced — shorts either pay in the same low band, share a publicist across a shorts block, or use the festival press office (inference from the Sundance Q&A and Screen Daily block-screening cost-splitting practice).

### Q3 — What a festival publicist actually does for a short at Sundance/SXSW/Tribeca
SUMMARY: Deliverables are: press-material prep and angle development, targeted pitching to press attending the festival, interview scheduling and press-line staffing at the premiere, screener management, and coverage tracking — with the honest expectation that shorts rarely get trade reviews, so the win condition is director profiles, 'shorts to watch' roundups, and craft/interview pieces rather than Variety/Deadline reviews. Several strategically placed articles beat one big-name outlet.
DETAIL: From the Sundance publicist Q&A: publicists gather stills/posters/trailers/press kits, coordinate press access, staff premiere press lines with photo and interview ops, pitch outlets, and assemble coverage; smaller films' publicists often work remotely or attend only opening days when press density peaks. Sundance's shorts-programmer advice: a good publicist 'will help manage expectations' and knows 'several strategically placed articles are often more valuable than one piece in a big name outlet.' The Film Collaborative adds: strategic plan, knowing which press are attending, screener distribution, coordination with sales agents. Reality check on trade coverage for shorts — See-Through Films' Kathleen McInnis: 'Getting press coverage for shorts in phase one is next to impossible unless you have a celebrity attachment.' Filmmaker Magazine's 25 New Faces list — the single most career-relevant press placement for an emerging director — explicitly positions itself as 'a genuine place for discovery' and NOT 'another publicist-driven list,' so it is earned by the work and festival visibility, not bought. Online-premiere placement (Vimeo Staff Pick Premiere, Short of the Week, a Vulture/NYT Op-Docs-style outlet for docs) is typically brokered after the festival run, and publicists or the filmmaker can pitch it directly (see Q5).

### Q5 — Timing of hiring, and the DIY path if you skip PR
SUMMARY: Hire within 1–2 months of the festival screening, never before acceptance — for Sundance that means the window opens with the late-Nov/early-Dec acceptance call, so press materials must exist BEFORE you're accepted. The DIY substitute is: a complete EPK built now, the festival's own press office as your pitch channel during the run, and the shorts-native online ecosystem (Short of the Week, Vimeo Staff Pick Premieres, Omeleto, Directors Notes) as the post-run distribution/press engine.
DETAIL: Timing (The Film Collaborative): budget for publicity at planning stage, but 'hire your publicist within 1-2 months of your festival screening' and 'create your press materials early.' DIY channel one — the EPK, standard components per Storydoc/Film Festival Secrets/Raindance guides: cover page with title/runtime/contacts, logline plus short/medium/long synopses (festivals and journalists lift these verbatim), director's statement, key cast/crew bios with headshots, 10–15 images mixing film stills and behind-the-scenes, technical specs (2.39:1, formats), full credits, laurels/awards list, trailer, and social links. DIY channel two — the festival press office: The Film Collaborative notes festival PR staff will 'point you towards journalists they think might be interested in your film' and give strategic guidance free. DIY channel three — post-run online premiere: Vimeo runs one Staff Pick Premiere per week with on- and off-platform editorial and marketing support; Vimeo's curators say they source shorts from Short of the Week, Directors Notes, Booooooom, and Nowness, so coverage on those sites feeds Staff Pick selection. In a Vimeo survey of Staff-Picked creators, 85% said it increased brand awareness, 68% said it led to paid/funded work, 61% said it helped launch their careers. Omeleto (large YouTube shorts channel) and Directors Notes (interview-led coverage) are the other standard post-run homes. ==================== PREMIERE_RULES ====================

### Q1 — What world / international / North American / US premiere actually mean
SUMMARY: There is no industry-wide statute; each festival defines premiere tiers in its own regulations, but the definitions converge. TIFF 2026 and the Berlinale FAQ publish the cleanest canonical definitions, and both hinge on the concept of a PUBLIC screening — private showings do not count.
DETAIL: TIFF 2026 Submissions FAQ (official PDF, verbatim): World Premiere = 'The Film has not had and will not have any public screening anywhere in the world before its scheduled presentation at the Festival.' International Premiere = 'The Film has only had a public screening in its country of origin (if the country of origin is not in North America)... (Please note: If a United States Film had a public screening only in the United States, it would qualify as an International Premiere.)' North American Premiere = public screenings in other countries but none yet in Canada or the US (Mexico/Caribbean screenings do not affect it). Canadian Premiere = screened elsewhere incl. the US, but never in Canada. Berlinale FAQ (2026 cycle): World premiere = 'The film has never been shown anywhere publicly.' International premiere = 'The film has only been released theatrically or presented at a festival in its country of origin, but nowhere else.' European premiere = shown outside Europe but never in Europe. 'US premiere' (used by US fests like Tribeca) = never publicly screened in the United States. Key mechanic: a 'public screening' at TIFF is one that was 'advertised and/or ticketed' with public and/or press attending — the definition every other question turns on. Note the useful TIFF quirk for a US film like Killer of Men: US-only festival screenings still leave it an 'International Premiere' in TIFF's system, so a small US premiere does not disqualify it from TIFF.

### Q3 — Do school showcases, cast-and-crew screenings, or WIP screenings break premiere status?
SUMMARY: Cast-and-crew, friends-and-family, and NDA'd private screenings do NOT break premiere status anywhere — TIFF says so explicitly and every definition keys off 'public screening'. School showcases are a gray zone: TIFF explicitly carves out 'compulsory school showcases', but Venice/Cannes/Berlinale's 'never shown publicly' language means a ticketed, advertised, open-to-the-public AFI showcase could technically void a world premiere there.
DETAIL: TIFF 2026 FAQ, verbatim: 'TIFF considers a public screening to be a screening where the public and/or press attended and the screening was advertised and/or ticketed. Exceptions include a screening where the public attended, but there was a non-disclosure agreement and no advertising, and/or a private friends-and-family/cast-and-crew screening. There may also be exclusions for compulsory school showcases. Please contact submissions@tiff.net if you are uncertain.' Sundance 2027 Rules (features language, showing the same principle): films 'exhibited theatrically at one or more private screenings for which tickets were or will not be made available to the general public retain eligibility'; WIP screenings 'at either public or private events' retain eligibility for incomplete versions (and a publicly ticketed WIP only disqualifies if the final version isn't significantly different) — and for shorts none of this even matters since Sundance shorts have no premiere requirement at all. The strict end: Venice requires the film 'must not have had any public or press screening'; Berlinale world premiere = 'never been shown anywhere publicly'; Cannes = 'not have been screened elsewhere than their home country' (a US school screening is in-country, so Cannes official-competition eligibility survives a domestic showcase — but Berlinale/Venice world-premiere STATUS would not if the showcase was public). PRACTICAL GUIDANCE for the AFI thesis showcase: if it is invitation/industry/NDA-style or a compulsory curricular screening, premiere status survives everywhere; if it is publicly ticketed and advertised (some AFI showcase nights are), treat the film as having had a US public screening — which still leaves TIFF (International Premiere), Cannes official competition (in-country screening allowed), and every no-premiere-required festival intact, but kills a Venice/Berlinale world premiere claim. Inference labeled as such: festivals almost never police unadvertised school screenings, but the safe play is to email the specific festival before claiming World Premiere. This determination matters most for Venice/Berlinale, which are likely off the table anyway on completion-window grounds (see the completion-window finding).

### Q4 — Online availability: trailer vs full film vs password-protected Vimeo
SUMMARY: Universal pattern across every festival checked: trailers and clips are always fine, password-protected private links are always fine, but a PUBLICLY viewable full film online is fatal at Cannes/Berlinale/Venice, geoblock-dependent at TIFF, category-killing at Palm Springs and Aspen, and irrelevant at Sundance (for recent shorts), Clermont, and HollyShorts.
DETAIL: Berlinale FAQ, verbatim: 'If the film was available on the internet or on VOD, it is no longer eligible... Sole exceptions are personal, password-restricted links, which were created for a festival submission process.' And: 'A trailer does not present any breach of our regulations.' This trailer/password-link doctrine is echoed everywhere — TIFF FAQ: 'Posting a trailer, clip, and/or actor reel online from your Film does not disqualify your Film,' and Tribeca/SXSW/TIFF all REQUIRE a password-protected streaming link as the submission format, so a private Vimeo screener never breaks anything. Full-film public availability by festival: Cannes (all 3 shorts sections): ineligible ('must not have been screened on the Internet'). Berlinale: ineligible, including prior online-festival streaming. Venice: ineligible ('must not have had any form of commercial distribution or streaming on the Internet'). TIFF: ineligible only if publicly viewable IN North America — 'Films that are publicly posted or exhibited online but geoblocked to a region outside of North America are still eligible.' Telluride: does not accept shorts released online. Aspen: 'Films publicly broadcast or made available online in the U.S. are ineligible,' though geo-restricted online-festival screenings are excused. Palm Springs ShortFest: previously-online films are relegated to the non-competitive 'Online Film Festival' category. SXSW: prior online release forfeits World Premiere labeling and is weighed in programming, but is not an absolute bar for shorts. Sundance 2027: a short completed after Dec 31, 2025 is eligible even if it is sitting on YouTube ('regardless of premiere status'; only pre-Jan-1-2026 public availability disqualifies). Clermont-Ferrand and HollyShorts: online availability is a non-issue. Sector-wide data (Stephen Follows, 10,549 festivals): only 11.1% of festivals actively reject already-online films; 16% explicitly accept them; 72.8% are silent. ACADEMY interplay: winning a designated festival award qualifies a short for the Oscar 'regardless of any prior public exhibition or distribution by nontheatrical means' (Rule 17 II.A.2) — online release never blocks the festival route; it only blocks the theatrical route (II.B, 15%-of-runtime allowance). BOTTOM LINE for this film: keep the full film off the public internet until the European-premiere question and the Oscar-qualifying run are resolved; trailer and password-protected screeners are safe everywhere.

### 1. Tiered-wave submission strategy and premiere-status sequencing
SUMMARY: The standard playbook is three waves over ~18 months: 3-6 months of world-premiere attempts at top-tier festivals, then a cascade to Oscar-qualifying mid-tiers and identity/regional fits, then reassess. Critically for shorts, premiere status matters less than folklore says: Sundance accepts shorts that have screened anywhere including online, while Berlinale Shorts and some European A-listers still demand premieres — so the thing to actually protect is the ONLINE release, not every prior screening.
DETAIL: Wave structure (filmfestivalfever.substack.com, a working programmer's newsletter): Phase 1 (months 1-3 of the campaign) = only selective prestige festivals where world premieres matter (Sundance, Cannes, Berlinale, Venice, TIFF, SXSW, Tribeca — acceptance rates 1-4%, described bluntly as treating unsolicited entries as 'revenue-generating fodder'); Phase 2 (months 3-6) = expand to regional, thematic, and identity-fit festivals while staying selective; Phase 3 (6+ months of rejections) = reassess fit. ShowRunHer and Indie Shorts Mag frame the same thing as a reach/realistic/safety tiered list mapped 9-12 months ahead of target festival dates. The conventional premiere hold: keep the film off all public platforms while the top tier is in play; Miralot's guide phrases it as protecting premiere status 'like it's money in the bank.' The practical 12-month top-tier loop: Sundance shorts deadline falls in Aug-Sep for the January festival, Cannes in winter for May, Venice/TIFF in spring for fall — one calendar year covers every major once. KEY NUANCE for a short (verified via Short of the Week's festival-eligibility survey): ~64% of top festivals accept films already online; Sundance's shorts rules explicitly allow prior festival screenings, TV, and internet exhibition; BFI London deprioritizes but doesn't exclude non-premieres; Berlinale rejects online films from Competition/Berlinale Shorts (accepts in Panorama/Forum/Generation); DOK Leipzig excludes anything published online. So the real discipline is: (a) never release online until the run is over, (b) hold the WORLD premiere for the top tier for 6-12 months, (c) after the premiere lands, screenings at mid-tier fests cost almost nothing strategically — cascade freely.

### 2. Real submission-budget numbers for a serious shorts campaign
SUMMARY: Average short-film entry fee is $30-55 depending on deadline tier (Stephen Follows' Sept 2025 study of 4,631 festivals). A focused serious run is 15-20 festivals at $1,000-2,000 in fees, $3,000-5,000+ all-in with deliverables and travel; a saturation campaign runs to $10,000. Several elite festivals are free to enter (Cannes shorts, Clermont-Ferrand, Oberhausen), which stretches the budget at the very top.
DETAIL: Stephen Follows (September 2025, dataset of 4,631 festivals, fees normalized to USD): shorts average $30-55 across deadline tiers vs $47-87 for features; four standard deadline tiers (early/regular/late/final) with early-bird saving $20-40 per entry; fees over $100 for a short flagged as exploitative. Aggregate campaign numbers from multiple 2025-26 guides (CMS Productions, Future Film Academy, Beverly Boy): focus on 15-20 carefully selected festivals ≈ $1,000-2,000 in fees alone; $3,000-5,000+ total once DCPs, closed-caption files, posters, and some travel are included; a common rule of thumb is 3-5% of production budget for festival costs. Filmfestivalfever's programmer-author: 'at least $1K to test the waters, up to $10K' for saturation (~100+ submissions), which he does not endorse. Free or near-free top-tier entries (Short Movie Club's 2025 survey): Cannes official shorts selection, Clermont-Ferrand, Oberhausen, Zinebi, Mar del Plata, Cinema Jove — the FIAPF-accredited slate alone can be covered for $400-600. Scam filter: skip festivals under 3-5 years old, anything with 'FEEDBACK'/'Lift-Off' in the name, and anything that emails discount codes unsolicited. For an AFI-pedigree prestige short aiming at an awards push, the realistic planning number is 30-50 submissions / $2,500-4,000 in fees over 18 months, weighted early toward free-and-elite entries.

### 4. The 18-24 month lifecycle: premiere → qualifying win → awards push → online release, and when to stop
SUMMARY: The successful arc runs: 6-12 months chasing a top-tier world premiere; 6-12 more months cascading through Oscar-qualifying and identity-fit festivals hunting a qualifying WIN; an FYC push into the October-December Academy shorts voting window; then an online premiere with a curated partner. The Academy's rule since 2018 means a qualifying-festival win qualifies the short regardless of prior online release, and Vimeo's Staff Pick Premieres accept films within 24 months of playing in competition at an Oscar-qualifying event — those two clocks define when to stop.
DETAIL: Arc for a film picture-locked January 2026: (a) Months 0-9 (mid-2026 into early 2027): world-premiere attempts at the majors plus free elite entries (Clermont-Ferrand, Oberhausen). (b) Once the premiere lands: cascade into the ~180 festivals on the Academy's Short Film Qualifying Festival List, because the qualification route for a short is winning a designated qualifying award at a listed festival (or a qualifying theatrical run). Typical total run: 12-18 months of active submissions, 18-24 months premiere-to-online.
