# Loglines — a sourced reference

*Compiled September 7, 2026 for the Killer of Men festival campaign. **These are found, not written.** Every entry is copied verbatim from a published source and carries that source's URL.*

## How to read the provenance labels

Most "famous loglines" circulating online were written by a blogger to illustrate a point, not by the production. That distinction matters when you are modelling your own, so every entry here is tiered:

| Label | Means |
|---|---|
| **official** | The studio, distributor, sales agent or festival programme published it |
| **database** | IMDb, TMDB, Letterboxd or MUBI — often distributor-supplied, sometimes user-written |
| **craft source** | A named screenwriting book, magazine or school quoting it as a teaching example |
| **reconstruction** | A blogger's own wording. Useful as craft, never as the film's own line |

Also labelled: **logline** (the real thing), **tagline** (poster copy — a different job), **synopsis opening** (the first line of a longer official synopsis, which usually functions as the de facto logline).

**Method.** Six research lanes, each with an adversarial verifier that fetched the source URL, checked the text was verbatim, downgraded dishonest tiers and corrected wordings. 168 entries survived; 122 confirmed outright, 32 corrected on verification, 14 left flagged as unverified. Zero were invented.

# Group A — historical drama in Killer of Men's territory

Twenty, weighted toward the two most useful registers: antebellum features, and **festival shorts**, which are the closest analogue to the task — loglines filmmakers wrote for submission rather than studios wrote for marketing.

## Antebellum and American slavery

### Sankofa (1993)

> On a photo shoot in Ghana, an American model slips back in time, becomes enslaved on a plantation and bears witness to the agony of her ancestral past.

**28 words · logline · database** — Letterboxd (TMDB synopsis field). [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/sankofa/)

Two concrete physical actions before the abstraction — the phrase 'ancestral past' is the last thing you read, so it lands as a payoff rather than a claim.

*For KOM:* The closest thematic twin in the lane. KOM's ancestral-roots material needs exactly this discipline: concrete action first, ancestry as the final noun, never the opening one.

### The Retrieval (2013)

> On the outskirts of the Civil War, a boy is sent north by a bounty hunter gang to retrieve a wanted man.

**22 words · logline · database** — Letterboxd (TMDB); same sentence used as the SXSW 2013 programme line. [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/the-retrieval/)

22 words, zero proper nouns, zero adjectives. Period as prepositional phrase, protagonist as 'a boy', engine as one transitive verb. The moral trap (a Black boy retrieving a Black man for bounty hunters) is implied, never explained.

*For KOM:* Best structural model in this lane for a short. KOM could run at this exact length and shape: setting phrase, unnamed protagonist, one forced action — and let the moral trap sit unstated.

### 12 Years a Slave (2013)

> TWELVE YEARS A SLAVE is based on an incredible true story of one man's fight for survival and freedom. In the pre-Civil War United States, Solomon Northup (Chiwetel Ejiofor), a free black man from upstate New York, is abducted and sold into slavery.

**43 words · synopsis opening · official** — Searchlight Pictures — official film site. [source](https://www.searchlightpictures.com/12yearsaslave/)

Frame-then-reversal: sentence one names the theme in plain nouns (survival, freedom); sentence two delivers the irreversible turn (free man abducted and sold) with no adjectives at all.

*For KOM:* Same two-beat shape KOM needs — one sentence of frame, one of irreversible turn. Note the reversal is stated flatly; the horror is in the fact, not the wording.

### The Birth of a Nation (2016)

> Set against the antebellum South, THE BIRTH OF A NATION follows Nat Turner (Nate Parker), a literate slave and preacher, whose financially strained owner, Samuel Turner (Armie Hammer), accepts an offer to use Nat's preaching to subdue unruly slaves. As he witnesses countless atrocities - against himself and his fellow slaves - Nat orchestrates an uprising in the hopes of leading his people to freedom.

**63 words · synopsis opening · official** — Searchlight Pictures — official film site. [source](https://www.searchlightpictures.com/thebirthofanation/)

Faith is stated as occupation ('a literate slave and preacher') and then weaponised by the antagonist. The moral contradiction is built into the premise; the word 'faith' never appears.

*For KOM:* KOM's faith thread can be a fact of the character's situation rather than a theme word. Let the antagonist's use of it apply the pressure — do not assert 'a reckoning with faith'.

### Nightjohn (1996)

> John is a man of many talents, including one forbidden skill: he can read. When he teaches a young slave girl named Sarny to read and write, she learns an unforgettable lesson about the power of words and the true meaning of freedom.

**43 words · logline · database** — Letterboxd (TMDB synopsis field). [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/nightjohn/)

One forbidden act carries the entire premise, and it is named in four words — 'he can read.' The colon does the dramatic work; the transgression is stated as a plain capability, which is what makes it land.

*For KOM:* Strong and directly applicable. KOM's equivalent forbidden act is the burial ceremony he stumbles on. Naming it that plainly — as a thing witnessed, not as 'a reckoning' — would sharpen the whole line.

### Daughters of the Dust (1991)

> At the dawn of the 20th century, a multi-generational family in the Gullah community on the Sea Islands off of South Carolina struggles to maintain their cultural heritage and folklore while contemplating a migration to the mainland, even further from their roots.

**42 words · logline · official** — Kino Lorber distributor synopsis, as published on the Gene Siskel Film Center programme page. [source](https://www.siskelfilmcenter.org/daughters-dust)

Geography does the thematic work. 'Even further from their roots' converts a physical relocation into the film's whole subject in six words — the theme is a consequence of the map, not a claim about the film.

*For KOM:* Same South Carolina ground as KOM. Shows how a place-fact can carry the ancestral theme so the logline never has to use the word 'ancestral' as an adjective on itself.

### Underground (2016)

> In 1857, a restless slave named Noah (Hodge) organizes a small team of fellow slaves on the Macon plantation outside Atlanta, and puts together a plan to run for their lives — 600 dangerous miles North — to freedom.

**39 words · logline · official** — WGN America press-release logline, quoted in Deadline (Yahoo syndication). [source](https://www.yahoo.com/video/underground-own-acquires-wgn-america-170043650.html)

One hard number — '600 dangerous miles' — does the work of scale, difficulty and stakes simultaneously. No adjective could have done all three.

*For KOM:* KOM has quantities available (1830; a fight to the death; a 13-minute frame). A single concrete number outperforms any adjective the producer is currently reaching for.

### Descendant (2022)

> Three miles north of downtown Mobile, Alabama sits Africatown, a community of around 2,000 residents and home to most descendants of the Clotilda, the last known ship illegally carrying enslaved Africans to the United States.

**35 words · synopsis opening · official** — Virginia Film Festival programme page. [source](https://virginiafilmfestival.org/films/descendant/)

Opens on a precise geographic fact ('three miles north of downtown Mobile') and lets the historical charge accumulate through stacked appositives. Ancestry becomes concrete through place and object — a town, a ship, a number.

*For KOM:* Strong. Shows how to make ancestry land without the words 'ancestral' or 'roots': attach it to a place, an object, a count. KOM's burial ground and the opponent's body are its equivalents.

### Free State of Jones (2016)

> In 1863, Mississippi farmer Newt Knight serves as a medic for the Confederate Army. Opposed to slavery, Knight would rather help the wounded than fight the Union.

**27 words · synopsis opening · database** — Rotten Tomatoes (distributor-supplied synopsis). [source](https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/free_state_of_jones)

Occupation-in-conflict: the job and the objection to the job, in two short sentences. Efficient, but the sentence has no pressure in it — nothing forces Knight yet.

*For KOM:* Weak model, included deliberately. It shows the lane's default convention (year + place + occupation) that KOM's producer should resist — KOM's man is forced, and a forcing verb beats a stative one.

### Django Unchained (2012)

> Set in the South two years before the Civil War, DJANGO UNCHAINED stars Academy Award®-winner Jamie Foxx as Django, a slave whose brutal history with his former owners lands him face-to-face with German-born bounty hunter Dr. King Schultz (Academy Award®-winner Christoph Waltz).

**42 words · synopsis opening · official** — The Weinstein Company official synopsis, first paragraph, as released to press. [source](https://thefilmstage.com/quentin-tarantinos-django-unchained-gets-official-synopsis/)

Dates the setting by proximity to a known event — 'two years before the Civil War' — instead of a bare year. The reader receives stakes and date in the same three words.

*For KOM:* Highly applicable. '1830 South Carolina' is inert; 'three decades before the war' makes the date do work. Consider whether KOM's date should imply pressure rather than merely locate.

## Festival shorts — programme loglines

### OURIKA! (2023)

> The long-dead Ourika, a Senegalese girl enslaved by a French aristocrat, is awoken in the eerie space between life and death, between body and soul, where she finds her way back to life and into liberation.

**36 words · logline · official** — Sundance Film Festival 2023 — Short Film Program lineup announcement (International Fiction Shorts). [source](https://www.sundance.org/blogs/2023-sundance-film-festival-announces-short-film-and-indie-episodic-program-lineup/)

Names the historical condition in an appositive rather than a clause — "a Senegalese girl enslaved by a French aristocrat" does period, race and power in eight words — then spends the rest of the sentence on the metaphysical space, not the plot. Ends on an abstract noun ("liberation") as the destination, which lets a short with little external plot still promise an arc.

*For KOM:* The single closest structural model in this lane: enslavement stated as an appositive so the sentence can spend its real estate on the liminal space between life and death — precisely the territory KOM enters at the burial ceremony.

### Grace (2024)

> Sixteen-year-old Grace prepares for her baptism in the rural 1950s South. When she learns she must repent before the ritual, she begins to question the budding romantic feelings she has toward her best friend, Louise.

**35 words · logline · official** — Sundance Film Festival 2024 — Short Film Program lineup announcement (U.S. Fiction Shorts). [source](https://www.sundance.org/blogs/2024-sundance-film-festival-announces-short-films-lineup-and-40th-edition-programming/)

Two-sentence build. Sentence one is pure setup: age, name, rite, place, period — no conflict at all. Sentence two opens on the hinge word "When" and converts the religious requirement itself into the dramatic problem. The faith element is not decoration; "she must repent" is the engine.

*For KOM:* Shows how to make a religious rite the mechanism of conflict rather than a backdrop, and proves a Black period short can spend a whole first sentence on place and period before any conflict arrives.

### Sweetheart (2025)

> In 1723 London, Thomas Neville is discovered cruising in the public toilets and forced to take refuge in a Molly House. There, he encounters a secret community as they prepare for a night of festivities.

**21 words · logline · official · corrected** — Sundance Film Festival 2025 — Short Film Program presented by Vimeo, lineup announcement. [source](https://www.sundance.org/blogs/2025-sundance-film-festival-unveils-short-film-program-presented-by-vimeo/)

Trusts one period-specific noun ("Molly House") to do all the historical work, unglossed. Twenty-one words for a costume drama. "Discovered" and "forced" are both passive constructions that make the protagonist's lack of agency the point.

*For KOM:* Two lessons: an unglossed period-specific term signals research and confidence, and deliberate passive voice can express coercion. KOM's man is *forced* into fighting — the grammar can carry that.

### Bay of Herons (2024)

> Calling on the strength of his ancestors, a young Mi'kmaq man reflects on the pain of bearing witness to the destruction of his homelands.

**24 words · logline · official** — Sundance Film Festival 2024 — Short Film Program lineup announcement (U.S. Fiction Shorts). [source](https://www.sundance.org/blogs/2024-sundance-film-festival-announces-short-films-lineup-and-40th-edition-programming/)

Opens on a participial phrase about ancestry, which sets the spiritual register before the protagonist even appears. Accepts an internal verb ("reflects") rather than faking external action — honest about being a mood piece and still selectable.

*For KOM:* Directly relevant on ancestral reckoning, but also a warning: "reflects on the pain of bearing witness" is soft, and KOM has actual physical action (a fight to the death) it should not trade away for this register.

### Unborn Biru (2023)

> A pregnant widow steals silver from a dead body in order to survive and feed her daughter. But the silver is cursed and has consequences for all of them, including the unborn.

**32 words · logline · official** — Sundance Film Festival 2023 — Short Film Program lineup announcement (Norway / Sámi). [source](https://www.sundance.org/blogs/2023-sundance-film-festival-announces-short-film-and-indie-episodic-program-lineup/)

Sentence one is a transgression against a corpse committed for a defensible reason — moral complexity in a single clause. "But" opens sentence two and turns the transgression supernatural. Guilt is made structural, not stated.

*For KOM:* Closest match to KOM's guilt problem: a character does something to or near a dead body, and the consequence is cosmic rather than legal. Note that the word "guilt" never appears — the structure carries it.

### Azheh (2023)

> A boy lives in a border village with Azheh. After Azheh's death, the boy tries to fulfill her will to be buried in his homeland.

**25 words · logline · official** — Sundance Film Festival 2023 — Short Film Program lineup announcement (International Fiction Shorts). [source](https://www.sundance.org/blogs/2023-sundance-film-festival-announces-short-film-and-indie-episodic-program-lineup/)

Twenty-five words, two sentences, one pivot on a death. Withholds who or what Azheh is — the ambiguity is the hook. The whole engine is a burial obligation, stated plainly with no adjectives at all.

*For KOM:* Proof that a burial obligation alone is enough to carry a logline, and that extreme brevity reads as confidence at festival level. If KOM's current logline is over-explaining, this is the counterweight.

### Suicide by Sunlight (2019)

> Valentina, a day-walking black vampire protected from the sun by her melanin, is forced to restrain her bloodlust to regain custody of her estranged daughters

**25 words · logline · official** — AFI FEST 2019 — official short films programme page. [source](https://fest.afi.com/2019/short-films-2019/suicide-by-sunlight/)

The premise's racial idea is buried inside a subordinate clause as physical mechanics ("protected from the sun by her melanin"), so the concept lands as worldbuilding rather than as a statement. Then a concrete, mundane want — custody — grounds the genre premise.

*For KOM:* Shows how to let the racial/historical idea sit in a clause and do its work implicitly, while the main verb carries an ordinary human want. Useful if KOM's logline is currently front-loading theme.

### Em & Selma Go Griffin Hunting (2025)

> In the Depression-ravaged countryside of 1930s America, adolescent girls are expected to fulfill a long-standing rite in which they hunt and slay a mythical beast of their mother's choosing.

**29 words · logline · official** — Sundance Film Festival 2025 — Short Film Program presented by Vimeo, lineup announcement. [source](https://www.sundance.org/blogs/2025-sundance-film-festival-unveils-short-film-program-presented-by-vimeo/)

Describes only the world's rule, never the plot. A single sentence that establishes period, social obligation and a ritual of sanctioned violence — the audience infers the story from the rule.

*For KOM:* Model for pitching a forced ritual of violence as a world-rule. KOM's underground prizefighting is exactly such a rule; describing the institution rather than the fight may be stronger than recounting events.

### We Were The Scenery (2025)

> In 1975, Hoa Thi Le and Hue Nguyen Che fled from Vietnam by boat and docked in the Philippines, where they were utilized as background extras during the filming of Apocalypse Now.

**26 words · logline · official · corrected** — Sundance Film Festival 2025 — Short Film Program presented by Vimeo, lineup announcement. [source](https://www.sundance.org/blogs/2025-sundance-film-festival-unveils-short-film-program-presented-by-vimeo/)

Flat, factual, no adjectives, real names — and the irony lands entirely on the last three words. The cold register makes the historical indignity hit harder than any editorializing would.

*For KOM:* Deadpan restraint as a strategy for atrocity material. Where KOM might be tempted toward heat, this shows understatement can be the sharper instrument.

### The Man Who Could Not Remain Silent (2024)

> February 27, 1993, Strpci, Bosnia and Herzegovina. A passenger train from Belgrade to Bar is stopped by paramilitary forces in an ethnic cleansing operation. As they haul off innocent civilians, only one man out of 500 passengers dares to stand up to them. This is the true story of a man who could not remain silent.

**56 words · synopsis-opening-as-logline · official · corrected** — Festival de Cannes — official film page (Short Film Palme d'Or 2024). [source](https://www.festival-cannes.com/en/f/the-man-who-could-not-remain-silent/)

Opens with a bare date-and-place slug line, like a screenplay header, which buys total period specificity for zero words of exposition. Then a hard number — "one man out of 500 passengers" — turns a moral situation into a countable ratio. Closes by folding the title back in as the thesis.

*For KOM:* The dateline opening is directly transplantable: "1830, South Carolina." as its own sentence would give KOM its period free. Also models moral stakes stated as arithmetic rather than adjectives.

# Group A, supplement — the fight films and the wider 19th century

The structural cousins: films built on organised violence and a man's reckoning with it, plus period drama beyond the American South.

### Raging Bull (1980)

> In the 1940s Bronx, middleweight boxer Jake LaMotta fights his way to the championship. But the rage and violence that propel him to the top threaten to destroy everything outside the ring—including his marriage, and his relationship with his brother and manager, Joey.

**43 words · logline · database · corrected** — MUBI. [source](https://mubi.com/en/us/films/raging-bull)

Pivots on "But" so that the exact quality that wins in the ring is named as the thing that destroys outside it. One trait, two consequences - the cleanest way to make a fight film sound like a character study.

*For KOM:* The "same trait, opposite consequence" hinge is portable. KOM's violence earns survival inside the ring and guilt outside it; a "But" hinge would make that legible in one sentence.

### Fat City (1972)

> The story of two men, a veteran boxer who is down and out, and a young man who is just starting his life and boxing career. Their fighting careers cross paths as their lives and fortunes head in opposite directions.

**40 words · logline · database** — Letterboxd. [source](https://letterboxd.com/film/fat-city/)

Two men, one crossing, opposite vectors. The premise is pure geometry - no incident is described at all, and none is needed.

*For KOM:* Moderate. If KOM's protagonist and his fallen opponent can be posed as two trajectories that intersect once, that shape carries a short film without plot summary.

### A Prayer Before Dawn (2017)

> Based on the international best-seller, A Prayer Before Dawn is the true story of Billy Moore, a troubled young British boxer sent to one of Thailand's most notorious jails. Refusing to die inside prison, Billy becomes a student of the lethal art of Muay Thai Boxing, and in the process, finds a brotherhood that will guide him on an incredible journey to redemption.

**63 words · logline · official** — Festival de Cannes official film page (2017 Midnight Screening). [source](https://www.festival-cannes.com/en/f/a-prayer-before-dawn/)

"Refusing to die inside prison" - a participial clause that converts a passive circumstance (he was sent there) into an active want, which is what the first sentence lacked.

*For KOM:* Useful fix for KOM's passivity problem. "An enslaved man is forced into..." is all circumstance; a "Refusing to ___" clause gives the character a verb inside a situation he did not choose.

### Warrior (2011)

> Two brothers face the fight of a lifetime – and the wreckage of their broken family – within the brutal, high-stakes world of Mixed Martial Arts (MMA) fighting in Lionsgate's action/drama, WARRIOR.

**33 words · logline · official · corrected** — Lionsgate official synopsis (opening line), reproduced on AceShowbiz. [source](https://collider.com/warrior-release-date-synopsis/)

Puts the bout and the wound in apposition inside one clause - "the fight of a lifetime - and the wreckage of their broken family" - so the sport IS the unfinished family business, not a backdrop to it.

*For KOM:* The single strongest structural model in this lane: one em-dashed apposition can bind the prizefight to the ancestral/inherited wound so the reader knows both are the same story.

### The Woman King (2022)

> The Woman King is the remarkable story of the Agojie, the all-female unit of warriors who protected the African Kingdom of Dahomey in the 1800s with skills and a fierceness unlike anything the world has ever seen.

**37 words · logline · official** — Sony Pictures official film page. [source](https://www.sonypictures.in/movies/woman-king)

Names the historical institution first and the individual second, betting that the unfamiliar collective noun ("the Agojie") is itself the hook. Sells rarity - a thing you have not seen - rather than plot.

*For KOM:* Moderate. If KOM's underground plantation prizefighting circuit has a specific historical name or practice, leading with the institution rather than the protagonist is a live option for a 13-minute film with limited plot to spend.

### Camp de Thiaroye (1988)

> Co-directed and co-written by Ousmane Sembène and Thierno Faty Sow, Camp de Thiaroye excavates a harrowing postwar tragedy from 1944: the Thiaroye Massacre, in which France murdered hundreds of West African veterans for demanding unpaid wages and protesting unfair treatment.

**40 words · logline · official · corrected** — Harvard Film Archive programme note. [source](https://harvardfilmarchive.org/calendar/camp-de-thiaroye-2023-0)

Uses "excavates" as the verb for the film itself - the film is an act of retrieval, not a story about an event. Then the colon delivers the buried fact plainly.

*For KOM:* Model for a short with a historical charge: let the verb describe what the film DOES to history (excavates, unearths, disinters), which for KOM sits naturally next to a burial ceremony.

### Ceddo (1977)

> The Ceddo try to preserve their traditional African culture against the onslaught of Islam, Christianity, and the slave trade. When King Demba War sides with the Muslims, the Ceddo kidnap his daughter, Princess Dior Yacine, to protest their forcible conversion to Islam.

**42 words · logline · database** — MUBI. [source](https://mubi.com/en/us/films/ceddo)

Sentence one states the three-way collision as a single pressure (ancestral tradition vs. imported faith vs. the slave trade); sentence two gives the concrete act that dramatises it. Theme first, incident second - the reverse of the usual order, and it works because the theme is the conflict.

*For KOM:* Closest thematic sibling in the whole lane. Shows you can name faith, ancestry and slavery together in one clause without abstraction, then land a single physical act. KOM's logline could state the collision (ancestral roots / imported faith / forced violence) then give the burial ceremony as the act.

### Yeelen (1987)

> A young man with magical powers journeys to his uncle to request help in fighting his sorcerer father.

**18 words · logline · database · corrected** — MUBI. [source](https://mubi.com/en/us/films/brightness)

Eighteen words, one journey, one antagonist, and the antagonist is the father. Patrilineal conflict stated as the whole plot.

*For KOM:* Moderate. Demonstrates how little is needed when the conflict is ancestral - naming the relation (father, forebear, the dead) is itself the stakes, no explanation required.

### Cold Mountain (2003)

> At the dawn of the Civil War, the men of Cold Mountain, North Carolina, rush to join the Confederate army. Ada has vowed to wait for Inman, but as the war drags on and letters go unanswered, she must find the will to survive while desperately struggling to hold onto her family's farm. Inman's long journey home takes him through the crumbling confederacy, as he meets people of all walks of life who both aid and hinder his mission.

**80 words · logline (official distributor synopsis, three sentences) · official** — Miramax official film page. [source](https://www.miramax.com/movie/cold-mountain/)

Opens with a period marker plus a specific place name in the same clause ('At the dawn of the Civil War, the men of Cold Mountain, North Carolina'), then splits into two parallel survival stories, one static and one in motion.

*For KOM:* The compressed time-and-place opener is exactly what KOM needs for '1830 South Carolina' — one clause, no throat-clearing, then straight into the person. Note it names a specific place, not a state; a named plantation or county would land harder than the region.

### The Nightingale (2018)

> THE NIGHTINGALE is a meditation on the consequences of violence and the price of seeking vengeance.

**16 words · logline (opening line of the official synopsis; functions as the de facto logline) · official** — IFC Films official film site (thenightingale.movie), Synopsis page. [source](https://www.thenightingale.movie/synopsis/)

Leads with theme, not plot. Names the film, states what it is a meditation ON, and puts two abstract nouns in tension (consequences of violence / price of vengeance) before any character, date or place appears. The plot paragraph then follows underneath.

*For KOM:* The single closest model in this lane. KOM is also a violence-and-reckoning film with an abstract spine; a one-line thematic header — 'KILLER OF MEN is a...' — followed by the 1830 South Carolina plot paragraph gives programmers the meaning first and the mechanics second.

### Hostiles (2017)

> Written and directed by Scott Cooper, Hostiles takes place in 1892 and tells the story of an Army Captain (Christian Bale) who reluctantly agrees to escort a dying Cheyenne chief (Wes Studi) and his family back to tribal lands. On the journey, they meet a widow (Rosamund Pike) whose family was murdered on the plains and offer their help. As the former rivals make their way from an isolated Army outpost in New Mexico to the grasslands of Montana, their relationship moves from antagonism to compassion, demonstrating humans' capacity for change.

**92 words · logline (official distributor synopsis) · official** — Entertainment Studios official synopsis page (Powster-hosted official film site). [source](https://app.powster.com/independentmovies/hostiles/us/synopsis/)

States the arc out loud in the final clause — 'moves from antagonism to compassion, demonstrating humans' capacity for change.' Most loglines withhold the ending; this one sells the transformation as the product.

*For KOM:* Permission to name the arc. KOM's ending is a reckoning, not a twist, so spelling out the movement (forced killer to mourner) costs nothing and tells a programmer what the 13 minutes are for.

### The Keeping Room (2014)

> In this radically reimagined American Western set towards the end of the Civil War, Southerner Augusta (Brit Marling, Arbitrage, The East) encounters two renegade, drunken soldiers (Sam Worthington, Avatar & Kyle Soller, BBC's "Poldark") who are on a mission of pillage and violence. After escaping an attempted assault, Augusta races back to the isolated farmhouse that she shares with her sister Louise (Hailee Steinfeld, True Grit, Pitch Perfect 2) and their female slave Mad (newcomer Muna Otaru.) When the pair of soldiers track Augusta down intent on exacting revenge, the trio of women are forced to take up arms to fend off their assailants, finding ways to resourcefully defend their home––and themselves––as the escalating attacks become more unpredictable and relentless.

**112 words · logline (official distributor synopsis, three sentences) · official** — Drafthouse Films official title page. [source](https://watch.drafthousefilms.com/products/the-keeping-room)

Opens by declaring its genre revision — 'radically reimagined American Western' — which tells a programmer where to file it and why it is not the thing they have already seen. Everything after that is siege mechanics.

*For KOM:* Highest situational overlap in this lane: Civil War-era South Carolina, an enslaved character inside the ensemble. The borrowable move is the genre-revision flag up front. KOM is a plantation film that is also a fight film; saying so in the first six words pre-empts the programmer's assumption about what an 1830 plantation short will be.

### Sweet Country (2017)

> Inspired by real events, Sweet Country is a period western set in 1929 in the outback of the Northern Territory, Australia. When Aboriginal stockman Sam kills white station owner Harry March in self-defence, Sam and his wife Lizzie go on the run. Eventually, for the health of his pregnant wife, he decides to give himself up. He is put on trial in the courtroom of Judge Taylor. But will justice be served?

**135 words · logline (official distributor synopsis) · official · corrected** — Samuel Goldwyn Films official film page. [source](https://www.labiennale.org/en/cinema/2017/program-cinema-2017/warwick-thornton-sweet-country)

Identifies the protagonist by race and age in the first six words because the racial dynamic IS the plot; then states the killing plainly and immediately reframes it — 'in self-defence' followed by 'wanted criminal for the murder of a white man.' The gap between what happened and what it is called is the film.

*For KOM:* Closest thematic sibling: an Indigenous man, a killing he did not choose, and a white system that names it for him. KOM should likewise make the gap explicit — what the fight IS versus what it is called by the men who staged it.

### The Proposition (2005)

> With the Australian outback circa 1880s serving as the backdrop, comes the story of the Burns Brother gang. Captain Stanley attempts to bring an end to violence by offering a proposition to Charlie Burns: he has nine days to kill his psychopathic brother, otherwise his younger brother will be hung.

**50 words · logline (short synopsis as published on MUBI) · database** — MUBI film page. [source](https://mubi.com/en/us/films/the-proposition)

The whole second sentence is a stated deal with a clock and a price — nine days, one brother for another. A logline built on an explicit bargain needs no adjectives to generate dread.

*For KOM:* KOM's premise is also a coerced bargain. Naming its terms — what he is made to do, by when, and what happens if he refuses — converts 'forced into underground prizefighting' from a situation into a countdown.

### First Cow (2019)

> A taciturn loner and skilled cook (John Magaro) has traveled west and joined a group of fur trappers in Oregon Territory, though he only finds true connection with a Chinese immigrant (Orion Lee) also seeking his fortune; soon the two collaborate on a successful business, although its longevity is reliant upon the clandestine participation of a nearby wealthy landowner's prized milking cow.

**62 words · logline (the story sentence of the official distributor synopsis; programme-note register) · official** — A24 official film page. [source](https://a24films.com/films/first-cow)

One long sentence built on two hinges — 'though' and 'although' — so the premise keeps complicating itself without a second sentence. Character is given by trait not name ('a taciturn loner and skilled cook'), and the whole thing lands on the one absurd concrete object the plot depends on.

*For KOM:* Trait-not-name is useful: 'an enslaved man' is already how KOM's protagonist reads, and the logline should keep him defined by condition and skill rather than by a proper noun a programmer won't retain.

### The Revenant (2015)

> Inspired by true events and winner of three Oscars® (actor, cinematography and directing), "The Revenant" follows the story of legendary explorer Hugh Glass (DiCaprio) on his quest for survival and justice. After a brutal bear attack, Glass is left for dead by a treacherous member of his hunting team (Tom Hardy). Against extraordinary odds, and enduring unimaginable grief, Glass battles a relentless winter in uncharted terrain.

**53 words · logline (story portion of the official studio synopsis; awards framing removed) · official · corrected** — 20th Century Studios official film page. [source](https://www.20thcenturystudios.com/movies/the-revenant)

Names the want in three words — 'survival and justice' — then withholds the antagonist's motive entirely. The betrayal is stated as event, not explained, which is why it still reads as a hook after the film is famous.

*For KOM:* Pairing a physical want with a moral one ('survival and justice') in a single phrase. KOM's man wants to live through the fight and to be right with the dead; naming both in one clause is tighter than a sentence each.

### The Assassination of Jesse James by the Coward Robert Ford (2007)

> Notorious outlaw Jesse James is a hero to many including the young and zealous Robert Ford. A wanted man with big money riding on his capture, James' demise may ultimatley come from within his own ranks.

**36 words · logline (distributor-supplied short synopsis as published on MUBI) · database · corrected** — MUBI film page. [source](https://mubi.com/en/us/films/the-assassination-of-jesse-james-by-the-coward-robert-ford)

Puts the idoliser and the idol in the same sentence so the betrayal is implied before it is stated, then lets the title do the spoiler work. The logline can be coy precisely because the title is not.

*For KOM:* Title-and-logline as one unit. 'Killer of Men' already carries a verdict; the logline can lean on it rather than repeating it, and spend its words on the burial ceremony instead.

# Group B — the loglines famous for being loglines

The canon as taught. Note how many are craft-source or database rather than official: the studio rarely publishes a true logline, so what gets taught is often a screenwriting teacher's compression. That is not a flaw — those compressions are the craft — but it should be labelled, and here it is.

### Die Hard (1988)

> A cop comes to L.A. to visit his estranged wife and her office building is taken over by terrorists.

**19 words · logline · craft source** — Blake Snyder, Save the Cat! (Michael Wiese Productions, 2005), Chapter One — quoted in Indie Cinema Academy's chapter summary. [source](https://indiecinemaacademy.com/save-the-cat-screenwriting-book-chapter-one-summary/)

Irony as engine: a small domestic errand (visit the estranged wife) is rammed into a siege. Two incompatible registers in one sentence, and the emotional stake is pre-loaded before the action starts.

*For KOM:* The single most-taught logline in English. If the board wants a model for 'ordinary intention meets catastrophic interruption,' this is the reference text.

### Pretty Woman (1990)

> A businessman falls in love with a hooker he hires to be his date for the weekend.

**17 words · logline · craft source** — Blake Snyder, Save the Cat!, Chapter One — quoted in Indie Cinema Academy's chapter summary. [source](https://indiecinemaacademy.com/save-the-cat-screenwriting-book-chapter-one-summary/)

Same irony test as Die Hard: a transaction becomes a romance. The contradiction is the whole pitch — no plot mechanics, no third act, just the impossible pairing.

*For KOM:* Snyder's companion example to Die Hard; useful as a matched pair showing the same craft move in two opposite genres.

### Blind Date (1987)

> She's the perfect woman — until she has a drink.

**9 words · tagline · craft source** — Blake Snyder, Save the Cat! — quoted in Musicbed's 'Lessons from Hollywood: Loglines'. [source](https://www.musicbed.com/articles/filmmaking/writing/lessons-from-hollywood-loglines/)

A turn on a hinge word ('until'). Setup and reversal in nine words.

*For KOM:* Included precisely because it is the classic category confusion: this is poster copy that Snyder cites inside a discussion of loglines, and bloggers downstream now list it as a logline. Flagged as TAGLINE.

### The Retreat (unproduced spec)

> A just-hired employee goes on a company weekend and soon discovers someone's trying to kill him.

**16 words · logline · craft source** — Blake Snyder, Save the Cat! — Snyder's own spec logline, quoted in Indie Cinema Academy and Musicbed. [source](https://indiecinemaacademy.com/save-the-cat-screenwriting-book-chapter-one-summary/)

Snyder pitching himself. The value here is that it is a logline for a script that was never made — so it is provably the writer's own sales wording, uncontaminated by later marketing.

*For KOM:* Rare specimen: a logline with no film behind it, therefore no studio copy to confuse it with. Clean example of the form as a selling instrument.

### Alien (1979)

> A deadly Alien is loose aboard a stranded spaceship.

**9 words · logline · craft source · unverified** — StudioBinder, 'How to Write a Film Treatment' — StudioBinder's own wording. [source](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/how-to-write-film-treatment/)

Radical subtraction. No character names, no plot beats, no third act — just threat plus enclosure. StudioBinder uses it to argue that a logline's job is the idea, not the story.

*For KOM:* The shortest defensible logline in the canon. Good stress-test for any board debating how much can be cut.

### Speed (1994)

> Die Hard on a bus

**5 words · pitch · database · unverified** — Wikipedia, Speed (1994 film) — the elevator pitch used to sell Graham Yost's script. [source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_(1994_film))

The 'X in Y' grammar applied to a template rather than a title — by 1994 'Die Hard in a ___' had become a standing formula (Die Hard on a plane, in a submarine, in the subway).

*For KOM:* Shows what the shorthand costs: Jan de Bont's first reaction to the pitch was that it sounded boring. A pitch that compresses too far can lose the thing that makes the film work.

### Jaws (1975)

> When a Great White shark terrorizes a quiet beach community, the town's police chief must overcome his crippling fear of the water and join forces with a grizzled shark hunter and a daredevil oceanographer to hunt it down and kill it to save the town.

**45 words · logline · craft source · unverified** — Barri Evins, 'BREAKING & ENTERING: I Want To Taste Your Logline', Script Magazine. [source](https://scriptmag.com/features/breaking-entering-want-taste-logline)

Every noun is doing work: 'grizzled', 'daredevil', 'crippling'. Evins's argument is that a logline should have flavour — specific adjectives that let a reader taste the movie — rather than being maximally short.

*For KOM:* The counter-argument to StudioBinder's nine-word Alien. Same lane, opposite doctrine: 45 words, and a working producer says that's right. Useful for a board that needs to decide which school it belongs to.

### E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial (1982)

> A lonely ten-year-old boy befriends a kindly alien stranded on Earth, takes him home and struggles to keep his new friend from being discovered by his mother or captured by government agents, ultimately realizing that he must help his friend go home in order to save him.

**47 words · logline · craft source** — Barri Evins, 'BREAKING & ENTERING: I Want To Taste Your Logline', Script Magazine. [source](https://scriptmag.com/features/breaking-entering-want-taste-logline)

Carries the emotional reversal into the logline itself — 'ultimately realizing that he must help his friend go home in order to save him' states the theme, which most loglines refuse to do.

*For KOM:* A rare case of a logline that includes the arc rather than only the premise. Directly relevant if the board is weighing whether to state theme.

### Se7en (1995)

> A seasoned homicide detective about to retire and the hotshot young detective hired to replace him, are forced to team in a gruesome hunt for a serial killer whose crimes are based on the Seven Deadly Sins, only to discover that they too, are part of his diabolical plan.

**49 words · logline · craft source** — Barri Evins, 'BREAKING & ENTERING: I Want To Taste Your Logline', Script Magazine. [source](https://scriptmag.com/features/breaking-entering-want-taste-logline)

Withholds the twist while promising one — 'only to discover that they too, are part of his diabolical plan' sells the ending without spending it.

*For KOM:* Direct comparison available: Final Draft's article gives IMDb's much shorter Se7en summary for the same film. Two published versions, 49 words vs 26, one by a producer and one lifted from a database.

### The Godfather (1972)

> The aging patriarch of an organized crime dynasty in postwar New York City transfers control of his clandestine empire to his reluctant youngest son.

**24 words · logline · database** — IMDb one-line plot summary. [source](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0068646/)

Whole-film compression through a single verb, 'transfers'. The word 'reluctant' is the entire tragedy — one adjective carrying Michael's arc.

*For KOM:* Probably the most-copied database line in screenwriting teaching. Widely reprinted as if it were a studio logline; it is not.

### Toy Story (1995)

> A cowboy doll is profoundly threatened and jealous when a new spaceman action figure supplants him as top toy in a boy's room.

**23 words · logline · database** — IMDb one-line plot summary, reprinted verbatim by NFI. [source](https://www.nfi.edu/logline-examples/)

States the emotion, not the plot. 'Profoundly threatened and jealous' is a character-interior logline — unusual, and the reason this one is taught.

*For KOM:* Frequently held up as proof that a logline can lead with feeling rather than incident.

### The Matrix (1999)

> A computer hacker learns from mysterious rebels about the true nature of his reality and his role in the war against its controllers.

**23 words · logline · database** — IMDb one-line plot summary, reprinted verbatim by NFI. [source](https://www.nfi.edu/logline-examples/)

Sells mystery by naming it rather than revealing it — 'the true nature of his reality' promises a reveal without spending it. The film's own marketing used the same withholding strategy.

*For KOM:* The standard teaching example for withholding a premise while still promising one.

### The Silence of the Lambs (1991)

> A young F.B.I. cadet must confide in an incarcerated and manipulative killer to receive his help on catching another serial killer who skins his victims.

**25 words · logline · database** — IMDb one-line plot summary, reprinted by StudioBinder. [source](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/write-compelling-logline-examples/)

Two antagonists, one of whom is the ally. The word 'confide' does more than 'consult' would — it makes the alliance intimate and therefore dangerous.

*For KOM:* Canonical teaching example of the reluctant-alliance structure.

### The Sixth Sense (1999)

> Following an unexpected tragedy, child psychologist Malcolm Crowe meets a nine year old boy named Cole Sear, who is hiding a dark secret.

**23 words · logline · database** — TMDB, Overview field. [source](https://www.themoviedb.org/movie/745-the-sixth-sense)

A logline built entirely around a thing it will not say. 'A dark secret' is a placeholder, and the film's commercial value depended on keeping it one.

*For KOM:* The textbook case of a logline constrained by a twist — it must sell the film without spoiling it, and the compromise is visible in the vagueness.

### The Truman Show (1998)

> In a picture-perfect seaside town, an insurance salesman begins to realize that his entire existence may be staged and observed by a vast unseen audience as part of a long-running real-time reality TV show.

**34 words · logline · database** — TMDB, Overview field. [source](https://www.themoviedb.org/movie/37165-the-truman-show)

Withholds nothing and still works, because the interest is not 'what is happening' but 'what will he do'. Contrast directly with The Sixth Sense above, which had to hide its premise.

*For KOM:* Best example on the list of a high-concept premise strong enough to be given away entirely in the logline.

### Parasite (2019)

> All unemployed, Ki-taek's family takes peculiar interest in the wealthy and glamorous Parks for their livelihood until they get entangled in an unexpected incident.

**24 words · logline · database** — TMDB, Overview field. [source](https://www.themoviedb.org/movie/496243-parasite)

Deliberate opacity — 'an unexpected incident' refuses to characterise the turn. For a film whose midpoint is its whole reputation, the logline's evasiveness is the strategy.

*For KOM:* Palme d'Or plus Best Picture; the reference case for a festival-to-Oscar run. Its logline is notably unhelpful, which is itself the lesson.

### Rocky (1976)

> Rocky Balboa is a Philadelphia club fighter who seems to be going nowhere. But when a stroke of fate puts him in the ring with a world heavyweight champion, Rocky knows that it's his one shot at the big time — a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity to go the distance and come out a winner!

**52 words · logline · database** — TMDB, Overview field. [source](https://www.themoviedb.org/movie/1366-rocky)

'Go the distance' is lifted straight from the film's own thematic vocabulary — the logline borrows the script's language rather than describing it from outside.

*For KOM:* Underdog structure at its most legible; the single most-imitated shape in festival narrative features.

### Chinatown (1974)

> Private eye Jake Gittes lives off of the murky moral climate of sunbaked, pre-World War II Southern California. Hired by a beautiful socialite to investigate her husband's extra-marital affair, Gittes is swept into a maelstrom of double dealings and deadly deceits, uncovering a web of personal and political scandals that come crashing together.

**53 words · logline · database** — TMDB, Overview field. [source](https://www.themoviedb.org/movie/829-chinatown)

Sells atmosphere before incident — 'murky moral climate of sunbaked, pre-World War II Southern California' is doing tonal work no plot summary could. Robert Towne's screenplay is the most-taught in the language and its logline is, tellingly, mostly weather.

*For KOM:* The canonical 'great screenplay' whose logline is the least impressive thing about it — useful evidence that logline quality and script quality are separable.

### Cast Away (2000)

> Chuck Noland, a top international manager for FedEx, and Kelly, a Ph.D. student, are in love and heading towards marriage. Then Chuck's plane to Malaysia crashes at sea during a terrible storm. He's the only survivor, and finds himself marooned on a desolate island. With no way to escape, Chuck must find ways to survive in his new home.

**59 words · logline · database** — TMDB, Overview field. [source](https://www.themoviedb.org/movie/8358-cast-away)

Spends its first sentence on what will be taken away. The romance exists in the logline solely so the island can cost something.

*For KOM:* Single-location survival premise — structurally relevant to the many low-budget one-hander features that reach festival boards.

### Erin Brockovich (2000)

> A twice-divorced mother of three who sees an injustice, takes on the bad guy and wins -- with a little help from her push-up bra. Erin goes to work for an attorney and comes across medical records describing illnesses clustered in one nearby town. She starts investigating and soon exposes a monumental cover-up.

**52 words · logline · database** — TMDB, Overview field. [source](https://www.themoviedb.org/movie/462-erin-brockovich)

Voice in the logline — 'with a little help from her push-up bra' is character rather than plot, and it tells you the film's register in seven words.

*For KOM:* Classic underdog-versus-institution shape, a staple of documentary and narrative festival submissions alike.

### Groundhog Day (1993)

> A cynical TV weatherman, along with his idealistic producer and his sardonic cameraman, is sent to report on Groundhog Day in the small town of Punxsutawney, where he finds himself repeating the same day over and over.

**37 words · logline · database** — TMDB, Overview field. [source](https://www.themoviedb.org/movie/137-groundhog-day)

Buries the hook. The premise — the loop — arrives in the final clause after two subordinate ones about the crew. A structural mistake worth studying.

*For KOM:* Blake Snyder files Groundhog Day under his 'Out of the Bottle' genre, but does not give it a logline in the Chapter One examples — so no Snyder wording exists for it. This database version is what circulates.

### Thelma & Louise (1991)

> Taking a break from their dreary lives, close friends Thelma and Louise embark on a short weekend trip that ends in unforeseen incriminating circumstances. As fugitives, both women rediscover the strength of their bond and their newfound resilience.

**38 words · logline · database** — TMDB, Overview field. [source](https://www.themoviedb.org/movie/1541-thelma-louise)

Euphemism as a problem — 'unforeseen incriminating circumstances' is so vague it drains the inciting incident of force. Callie Khouri's Oscar-winning original screenplay deserves better than its own database line.

*For KOM:* Instructive failure: a landmark script with a logline that names no antagonist, no goal and no jeopardy.

### Up (2009)

> Carl Fredricksen spent his entire life dreaming of exploring the globe and experiencing life to its fullest. But at age 78, life seems to have passed him by, until a twist of fate (and a persistent 8-year old Wilderness Explorer named Russell) gives him a new lease on life.

**49 words · logline · database** — TMDB, Overview field. [source](https://www.themoviedb.org/movie/14160-up)

Conspicuously omits the balloons and the house — the film's single most famous image is absent from its own summary. A reminder that database copy is written to inform, not to sell.

*For KOM:* Compare Pixar's own official page, which leads with the image the database version drops.

### Whiplash (2014)

> Andrew Neiman is an ambitious young jazz drummer, single-minded in his pursuit to rise to the top of his elite east coast music conservatory.

**24 words · synopsis opening sentence · official** — Sony Pictures Classics, official Whiplash synopsis. [source](https://sonyclassics.com/whiplash/)

Character-first, want-first. No antagonist in sentence one — Fletcher arrives in sentence three. The distributor is selling ambition before it sells cruelty.

*For KOM:* Official distributor copy, verified by direct fetch. Compare against the festival version below — same film, two official bodies, materially different emphasis.

### Moonlight (2016)

> A timeless story of human connection and self-discovery, Moonlight chronicles the life of a young black man from childhood to adulthood as he struggles to find his place in the world while growing up in a rough neighborhood of Miami.

**40 words · synopsis opening sentence · official** — A24 Films, official film page. [source](https://a24films.com/films/moonlight)

Leads with the register ('timeless story of human connection and self-discovery') before the particulars, then narrows to place. The distributor is positioning the film's universality first and its specificity second — a deliberate awards-season move.

*For KOM:* Genuinely official, from the distributor, verified by direct fetch. One of the few entries on this list where the wording provably belongs to the people who released the film.

### Legally Blonde (2001)

> An airhead blonde goes to Harvard Law School.

**8 words · logline · **reconstruction — someone wrote this to illustrate a point** · unverified** — NFI, 'Logline Examples: 74 Famous Examples You Should Know' — NFI's own wording, no source credited. [source](https://www.nfi.edu/logline-examples/)

Pure collision of category. Two nouns that do not belong in the same sentence, and the film is entirely the friction between them. No goal, no antagonist, no stakes — and it still works.

*For KOM:* The minimal case. If the board is testing how short a functioning logline can get, this and StudioBinder's nine-word Alien are the two floor specimens.

### Fargo (1996)

> Jerry, a small-town Minnesota car salesman is bursting at the seams with debt... but he's got a plan. He's going to hire two thugs to kidnap his wife in a scheme to collect a hefty ransom from his wealthy father-in-law. It's going to be a snap and nobody's going to get hurt... until people start dying. Enter Police Chief Marge, a coffee-drinking, parka-wearing - and extremely pregnant - investigator who'll stop at nothing to get her man. And if you think her small-time investigative skills will give the crooks a run for their ransom... you betcha!

**94 words · synopsis · database** — TMDB, Overview field (period Gramercy/PolyGram trailer copy). [source](https://www.themoviedb.org/movie/275-fargo)

Trailer voice, not logline voice — second person, exclamation marks, a punchline. Shows the difference between copy that sells a ticket and copy that sells a script.

*For KOM:* The clearest specimen on this list of MARKETING SYNOPSIS masquerading as a summary. If a board is auditing which of its own materials are loglines, this is the anti-model.

# The rules, from named authorities — including where they disagree

**Snyder's four elements** — A good logline must satisfy four requirements: irony - "The logline must be ironic and emotionally involving"; a compelling mental picture - "The logline must imply the entire movie"; audience and cost - "The logline must demarcate the target audience and potential production cost"; and a killer title - "The logline must include a title that cleverly communicates the story's substance."

*Blake Snyder, Save the Cat! (2005) - read via Wikipedia's summary of the book.* [source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Save_the_Cat!:_The_Last_Book_on_Screenwriting_You'll_Ever_Need)

**Sundance's 75-word ceiling** — "LOGLINE (75 words max) - A one- to two-sentence description of your story." Sundance Institute's Development Track application states this twice, making 75 words the hard ceiling and one-to-two sentences the expected form for the Institute's own intake.

*Sundance Institute - 2027 Development Track application.* [source](https://apply.sundance.org/prog/2027_development_track/)

**Nicholl: no logline, no entry** — "Entries lacking a logline on the application form will be disqualified." The same rules add that a script adapted from the entrant's own prior work "should be noted in the logline required on the application form" - so the logline is also the place where provenance is declared.

*Academy Nicholl Fellowships in Screenwriting - 2026-2027 Rules, Terms and Conditions (Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences).* [source](https://www.oscars.org/sites/oscars/files/2026-06/2026-2027%20Nicholl%20Rules%20Terms%20and%20Conditions%20(1).pdf)

**The 25-50 word band** — "25 to 50 words" is the working band: "Anything below 25 words - with a few exceptions as you'll read below - is closer to a tagline" and "Anything above 50 words is turning into a short synopsis." Ideally "one punchy sentence that leaves them wanting more."

*Final Draft - "101 Best Movie Loglines Screenwriters Can Learn From".* [source](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/101-best-movie-loglines-screenwriters-can-learn-from)

**ScreenCraft's one-sentence absolutism** — "A logline is a one-sentence outline of your film using only 35-45 words" and "It must be only one complete sentence. Any longer and it becomes a synopsis; any shorter and it becomes a tagline." A companion ScreenCraft piece instead tells writers to stick "as close to twenty-five words as you can."

*ScreenCraft - "How To Write Effective Loglines" and "The Simple Guide to Writing a Logline".* [source](https://screencraft.org/blog/how-to-write-effective-loglines/)

**The anti-formula position** — "A lot of people are going to try to sell you a logline generator or a logline formula, but that's all bullshit." Every tool or fix is only a jumping-off point; each has to adjust to the movie at hand rather than the movie bending to the template.

*No Film School - "How to Write a Logline for your Screenplay (Free Template)".* [source](https://nofilmschool.com/logline)

**Brevity over structure** — "Brevity and impact - not some rigid, arbitrary structure - is the key," and "going two or even three sentences is fine." Mark Sevi offers a template while explicitly refusing to credit it: "I apologize that I can't properly credit this because I don't remember where it came from."

*Mark Sevi, "The Screenwriter's Toolbox Part I: Loglines and Pitches" (Final Draft blog).* [source](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/the-screenwriters-toolbox-part-i-loglines-and-pitches)

**No names, no ending** — "No need to use the character's name. It provides no real value." And: "Don't give away the ending." The protagonist enters the logline as a type and a flaw, not as a proper noun the reader has no reason to care about yet.

*StudioBinder - "What is a Logline? [with FREE Logline Formula Template]".* [source](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-a-logline/)

**Kouguell's ten tips** — Use present tense. "Every word must do double duty. Less is more." Don't include characters' names. Avoid wordy phrases such as "It's a story about..." or "We follow the journey of..." Don't write a run-on sentence. Define the protagonist by profession, idiosyncrasies and/or flaws.

*Susan Kouguell (Script Magazine contributor), "Top Ten Logline Tips" - Screenwriter's Utopia.* [source](https://www.screenwritersutopia.com/article/a4d71de0)

**Hauge's story concept template** — "TITLE is a GENRE about HERO, a ROLE, who EMPATHY/SET UP. When HERO is OPPORTUNITY, s/he decides to NEW SITUATION/PRELIMINARY GOAL. But when CHANGE OF PLANS, s/he now must OUTER MOTIVATION/PRIMARY GOAL by HERO's PLAN as well as SECOND GOAL in spite of the fact that OUTER CONFLICT."

*Michael Hauge's story concept template, reproduced by Jill Williamson (attributed to storymastery.com).* [source](https://jillwilliamson.com/michael-hauges-story-concept-template/)

**High concept in a single sentence** — "A high concept movie is a genre film (action, suspense thriller, comedy, romantic comedy, horror, science fiction or family film) that is easily expressed in a single sentence." It must promise big conflict, carry familiar elements, and add one element we have never seen before.

*Michael Hauge, "Commercial Potential" - Story Mastery.* [source](https://www.storymastery.com/selling-your-story/commercial-appeal/)

**Truby: premise is the story in one sentence** — The premise is your story stated in one sentence: the simplest combination of character and plot, typically an event that starts the action, some sense of the main character, and some sense of the outcome of the story.

*John Truby, The Anatomy of Story, pp. 16-18 (via reader notes on Medium).* [source](https://www.beabrilliantwriter.com/anatomy-of-story-truby/)

**The catalogue constraint** — For festival intake a logline is "one or two sentences (usually about 25 words)"; the synopsis a catalogue asks for is typically "6-7 sentences (about 300 characters)." Where the form allows only 100 characters, "logline will stand for a synopsis." Count characters in the English text, not the original language.

*Angelica Lashuk, "Logline and Synopsis: Characteristics, Features, Requirements" - Festagent.* [source](https://festagent.com/en/journal/logline-and-synopsis)

**Logline is not tagline** — "A logline is simply a concise and punchy way of describing what your film is about" - it "teases the emotional energy of your film." A tagline is marketing copy ("In space, no one can hear you scream"); a logline exists to make someone want to read or watch.

*Sanj Krishnan, Production & Development Executive, Film London.* [source](https://filmlondon.org.uk/loglines-what-they-really-are-and-how-to-write-a-great-one)

**What the logline leaves out** — "Note what isn't included in the logline: The names of any of the characters. Thematic concerns. Emotional arcs. Subplots. Descriptions of particular set pieces." What remains is protagonist, antagonist, conflict and stakes, setting, and tone or genre - in "one concise sentence, two max."

*Sean P Carlin, "Foundations of Storytelling, Part 1: The Logline as Both a Sales and Writing Tool".* [source](https://www.seanpcarlin.com/storytelling-basics-part-1-logline/)

**Test it, and proofread it** — "A logline needs to be tested, and not just once but on a frequent and regular basis." Mechanics carry weight: "If you misspell a single word, use incorrect syntax or punctuation, employ a run-on sentence, or make any sort of demands, your logline will instead scream 'I'm barely literate and socially awkward!'"

*Jared Wynn, "How to Write a Logline (With Examples for Screenplays)" - InkTip.* [source](https://www.inktip.com/article_single.php?a_id=62)

**The unexpected is your friend** — "The Unexpected Is Your Friend." "Don't just tell us that your protagonist is a waiter - that's boring"; use contrast, e.g. "knife-juggling waiter" or "counselor addicted to gambling." Avoid proper names unless they carry historical significance or audience recognition, and don't open on setting alone.

*PremiumBeat, "The Do's and Don'ts of Writing a Logline for Your Film or Video Project".* [source](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/writing-logline-for-film-video-project/)

**All roads lead to outer motivation** — "It's ALWAYS a goal thing. Whenever you get stuck or lost in the thicket of your plot, or are having problems with a scene, all roads lead toward your hero's Outer Motivation." Hauge's five visible goals: "To win the love of another person or a competition", "To stop something bad from happening", "To escape a bad situation", "To deliver or transport an item of value", "To retrieve something of value and bring it to safety or possess it."

*Michael Hauge - Story Mastery / Film Courage interviews.* [source](https://www.storymastery.com/character-development/what-does-your-hero-want-outer-motivation/)

**Eight rules, one of which is break the rules** — "Write a logline, not a synopsis." "Don't write a tagline either." "Sell the main character." "Don't use names." "Think about your character's #goals." "Use the active voice." "Raise the stakes." "Tear up the rulebook." Structure: "When inciting incident happens, the main character decides to do central conflict against antagonist."

*Luke Leighfield, "How to Write a Logline (Step-by-Step Guide)" - Boords.* [source](https://boords.com/blog/write-logline)

**Austin: optional at entry, decisive after** — "How long should my synopsis be? It should be a brief summary or logline for your script. The synopsis is optional and not used for judging purposes." Separately, AFF's competition listing states that "Semifinalists and Finalists' loglines and contact information are also included in the annual Producer's Book, distributed to all AFF panelists as well as over 800 agents, managers, producers, and other industry professionals."

*Austin Film Festival - About AFF Script Competitions.* [source](https://austinfilmfestival.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/SPLAY-COMPETITION-FAQ.pdf)

### Where they contradict each other

- **Length.** ScreenCraft: "35–45 words… must be only one complete sentence." Final Draft: 25–50 words, under 25 is a tagline, over 50 is a synopsis. Sundance's own application: 75 words maximum, one to two sentences. Festagent, writing about festival catalogues specifically: about 25 words. Mark Sevi: "going two or even three sentences is fine."
- **Names.** StudioBinder, Kouguell and Boords all say do not name the protagonist. But 57% of the KOM-adjacent loglines here name them, and festival shorts name them most (62%). The no-names rule is a **spec-market** rule for contest readers; the festival-programme register names people.
- **Formula itself.** Hauge sells a fill-in template; No Film School calls logline formulas "bullshit"; Sevi offers a template and then apologises for it.

### The hard requirements, which do not conflict

- **Nicholl Fellowship:** "Entries lacking a logline on the application form will be disqualified."
- **Sundance Institute:** 75 words maximum, one to two sentences.
- **Austin Film Festival:** optional at entry — but semifinalists' and finalists' loglines are circulated to industry.

# What the corpus actually does — counted, not asserted

METHOD: counted over a named, auditable set of 30 KOM-adjacent entries — 9 antebellum (Sankofa, The Retrieval, Daughters of the Dust, Underground, Nightjohn, Free State of Jones, Beloved, Antebellum, Descendant), 8 fight/diaspora (Warrior, Undisputed, Ceddo, The Woman King, A Prayer Before Dawn, Raging Bull, Yeelen, Camp de Thiaroye), 13 shorts (OURIKA!, Grace, Runaway, Suicide by Sunlight, Dona Beatriz, Bay of Herons, Set Lam, Unborn Biru, Em & Selma, Sweetheart, The Last Ranger, Nakam, The Man Who Could Not Remain Silent). Taglines, craft rules and reconstructions excluded.

LENGTH. Mean 36.8 words, median 36. Shorts-only mean 36.2, median 35. Range 18 (Yeelen) to 62 (A Prayer Before Dawn). Sits inside the corpus's own 25-50 word rule and at half of Sundance's 75-word ceiling. Target 35.

SENTENCE COUNT. One sentence 14/30 (47%). Two sentences 14/30 (47%). Three or more 2/30 (7%). So 93% are one or two sentences — exactly Sundance's stated spec. Both 3+ entries are period shorts opening on a bare date-place fragment.

OPENS WITH SETTING. 12/30 (40%) overall — but antebellum 6/9 (67%), fight 1/8 (13%), shorts 5/13 (38%). Period pieces open on the date or place; fight films open on the character or situation. All 5 setting-first shorts are period pieces and 4 of them use a hard date. For a dated period short, setting-first is the norm, not a crutch.

NAMES THE PROTAGONIST. 17/30 (57%) — antebellum 5/9, fight 4/8, shorts 8/13 (62%). This contradicts the corpus's own craft lane, which tells writers three separate times not to use names (Kouguell; "No names, no ending"; the eight-rules entry). The no-names rule is a SPEC-MARKET rule. The festival-programme register names people, and shorts name them most. KOM is going to festivals, not to a contest reader: naming the man is permitted and probably right.

STATES THE IRONY OR THE COST. 22/30 (73%) — antebellum 5/9 (56%), fight 6/8 (75%), shorts 11/13 (85%). The single strongest correlate of a logline that works. Shorts state the cost at 85% because a short has no runtime in which to promise one later — the logline must contain it. This is the one element KOM's logline cannot omit.

COLON OR DASH. 6/30 (20%) — antebellum 3/9, fight 3/8, shorts 0/13. Zero of thirteen shorts use either. It is a feature-marketing device. Shorts get the same compression from the appositive comma (OURIKA!, Suicide by Sunlight) and the two-sentence "But" turn (Unborn Biru, Raging Bull). Do not reach for a dash.

ENDS ON THEME VS PLOT. Theme 10/30 (33%), plot or stakes 20/30 (67%). WARNING: of the 4 antebellum entries ending on theme, 3 land on the literal words "freedom", "roots" or "past". That is the most worn close in the lane — KOM ending on "freedom" would be invisible.

ENDS ON A WITHHELD REVEAL. 3/30 (10%) — The Last Ranger ("a terrible secret"), Beloved ("strange things start to happen..."), Antebellum ("before it's too late"). Withholding is far rarer than producers assume; 90% of these loglines tell you what happens.

PRESENT TENSE. 30/30 (100%). No exceptions in the entire adjacent set.

CARRIES A PROVENANCE OR CREDIT FRAME. 7/30 (23%) — "Based on the international best-seller" (A Prayer Before Dawn); "Co-directed and co-written by" (Camp de Thiaroye); "The Woman King is the remarkable story of"; "This short dramatic film follows" (Runaway); "in Lionsgate's action/drama, WARRIOR"; "Set in... inspired by the true story of" (Dona Beatriz); "This is the true story of" (The Man Who Could Not Remain Silent). All seven are the weakest constructions in the set — the frame eats words the story needs. Runaway spends its first six words on "This short dramatic film follows" and is the least effective antebellum short logline here. KOM has no source novel and no marquee attachment, therefore no frame to spend words on. Spend all 35 on the story.

THE COMPOSITE. The strongest KOM-adjacent loglines are present tense, ~35 words, one or two sentences, opening on a date or place because the film is period, naming the protagonist, carrying the historical premise in an appositive rather than a clause, stating the cost explicitly in the second sentence, using no colon and no dash, and closing on the specific consequence rather than on an abstraction. Six of the thirty do all of this at once: Sweetheart, Grace, OURIKA!, Unborn Biru, Bay of Herons, Free State of Jones.

# The six worth studying for Killer of Men

1. SWEETHEART (2025 short, official). "In 1723 London, Thomas Neville is discovered cruising in the public toilets and forced to take refuge in a Molly House. There, he encounters a secret community as they prepare for a night of festivities." STRUCTURE: dated place opener; a named man FORCED into a hidden world in sentence one; sentence two turns from the coercion to the ceremony he stumbles into. 36 words, two sentences, no dash, no colon. This is KOM's shape already built — forced into underground prizefighting / stumbles on the burial ceremony. Same two beats, same order, same length. Copy the skeleton, not the words.

2. GRACE (2024 short, official). "Sixteen-year-old Grace prepares for her baptism in the rural 1950s South. When she learns she must repent before the ritual, she begins to question the budding romantic feelings she has toward her best friend, Louise." STRUCTURE: sentence one plants the rite; sentence two has the rite demand something the protagonist cannot give. 35 words. Faith is the engine and is never named as a theme. Shows how a ceremony does dramatic work instead of being reported as subject matter — KOM's burial rite fits this slot exactly.

3. RAGING BULL (1980, database). "In the 1940s Bronx, middleweight boxer Jake LaMotta fights his way to the championship. But the rage and violence that propel him to the top threaten to destroy everything outside the ring—including his marriage, and his relationship with his brother and manager, Joey." STRUCTURE: the win in sentence one, the cost in sentence two on a "But". The cleanest cost clause in all 168 entries. The model for stating what the fighting COSTS without describing a single fight.

4. OURIKA! (2023 short, official). "The long-dead Ourika, a Senegalese girl enslaved by a French aristocrat, is awoken in the eerie space between life and death, between body and soul, where she finds her way back to life and into liberation." STRUCTURE: the entire period-and-bondage premise is loaded into a comma-bracketed appositive so the main verb is free to carry the metaphysical turn. 36 words, one sentence. The most transferable technique in the corpus — put "1830 South Carolina" and "enslaved" in an appositive so the main clause can spend itself on the burial.

5. BAY OF HERONS (2024 short, official). "Calling on the strength of his ancestors, a young Mi'kmaq man reflects on the pain of bearing witness to the destruction of his homelands." STRUCTURE: ancestors front-loaded as a participial phrase, not announced as a theme. 24 words, no proper names, ends on the wound. The only entry in 168 that puts ancestry into a logline without the word "ancestral" doing abstract duty.

6. UNBORN BIRU (2023 short, official). "A pregnant widow steals silver from a dead body in order to survive and feed her daughter. But the silver is cursed and has consequences for all of them, including the unborn." STRUCTURE: a corpse, something taken from it, a consequence reaching past the taker. Two sentences on a "But", 32 words, no names. The corpse-and-consequence mechanic compressed to one sentence plus a turn.

RUNNERS-UP: Nightjohn (1996) — "including one forbidden skill: he can read", the only antebellum entry built on a transgressive act of transmission rather than a run for freedom, and the closest register match if he wants an antebellum-lane voice. Free State of Jones — irony stated in seven words. Suicide by Sunlight — 25 words carrying premise and stakes entirely on an appositive.
